\section{Abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) achieve state-of-the-art accuracy in vision and language tasks but remain vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations, an unacceptable risk for safety-critical systems such as autonomous driving or medical imaging. In parallel, the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm provides scalable and modular computation by routing inputs to specialized subnetworks. However, while large homogeneous MoE models (e.g., GShard, Switch Transformer, Mixtral) demonstrate remarkable scalability, their robustness and verifiability, especially in heterogeneous settings, remain poorly understood.

Formal verification tools like $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN now enable provable robustness guarantees for neural networks, yet their integration with modular, multi-expert architectures is underexplored. Bridging empirical adversarial robustness and formal certification is essential for building verifiable AI systems that are both practical and theoretically sound.

This paper introduces MetaMoE, a heterogeneous, verifiable MoE architecture composed of Adversarially trained experts and a formally verified gating network. We study how robustness propagates across modules and how empirical robustness (under PGD attacks) correlates with certified robustness accuracy (CRA). The work contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A compositional robustness framework linking adversarial training and formal verification.
    \item Verification-aware CNN architecture designed for $\alpha - \beta$-CROWN.
    \item Empirical--formal correlation analysis showing $AA \approx CRA - \delta$.
    \item Scalability results showing 16.7\% faster incremental training and linear inference scaling
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results provide a unified empirical-formal perspective on robustness and scalability in heterogeneous MoE systems.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let:
\begin{equation}
    x \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}
\end{equation}

denote an input image, normalized to a fixed resolution $H = W = 32$. The goal of a heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) classifier is to predict a semantic class:

\begin{equation}
    y \in \{1, \ldots, C_{total}\}, \quad C_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i,
\end{equation}

where $C_i$ is the number of classes belonging to dataset/domain $i$, and $N$ is the total number of pretrained experts.

The MoE consists of two components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expert Model:}
    \begin{equation}
        E_i: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}, \quad y_i = E_i(x)
    \end{equation}
    where $y_i$ is the logit vector for domain $i$

    \item \textbf{Gating (Routing) Network:}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^N, \quad g = \mathcal{G}(x)
    \end{equation}
    where $g_i$ denotes the router logit for selecting expert $i$
\end{enumerate}

The router predicts a meta-class
\begin{equation}
    m^* = \arg\max_i g_i
\end{equation}
identifying the most suitable expert for the input.

\subsection{Adversarial Threat Model}

We consider an $L_\infty$-bounded adversary generating perturbations:
\begin{equation}
    \delta \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}
\end{equation}

Such that
\begin{equation}
    \|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon
\end{equation}

yielding adversarial examples
\begin{equation}
    x' = x + \delta.
\end{equation}

An MoE classifier is robust at $x$ if
\begin{equation}
    \arg\max_c \hat{y}_c(x') = y, \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)
\end{equation}
where $B_\varepsilon(x)$ is the closed $\varepsilon$-ball around $x$, and $\hat{y}$ is the MoE prediction defined below.

\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts Inference Rule}

Given router logits $g$, we consider a sparse top-$k$ selection mechanism. Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_k(g) = \{i_1, \ldots, i_k\}, \quad g_{i_1} \geq g_{i_2} \geq \cdots \geq g_{i_k},
\end{equation}
denote the indices of the selected experts.

Normalized mixing weights are computed as
\begin{equation}
    w_j = \frac{g_{i_j}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^k g_{i_\ell}}, \quad j = 1, \ldots, k.
\end{equation}

Each expert output $y_i$ occupies a dataset-specific region in the global logit vector. Using offsets
\begin{equation}
    o_i = \sum_{m<i} C_m,
\end{equation}

the final MoE logits $\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{total}}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_c = \begin{cases}
        w_j \cdot y_{i_j}[c - o_{i_j}], & \text{if } c \in \{o_{i_j}, \ldots, o_{i_j} + C_{i_j} - 1\} \\
        0, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The predicted class is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}^* = \arg\max_c \hat{y}_c.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Robustness Objectives}

\subsubsection{Expert Robustness}

An expert $E_i$ is $\varepsilon$-robust at $x$ if
\begin{equation}
    \arg\max y_i(x') = y \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x).
\end{equation}

Certified robustness accuracy (CRA) for expert $E_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \text{CRA}_i(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{|D_i|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_i} \mathbb{1}[\text{Verify}(E_i, x, \varepsilon) = \text{True}].
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Router Robustness}

Router robustness requires
\begin{equation}
    m^*(x') = m^*(x) \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)
\end{equation}

or in logit form, for all incorrect classes $j \neq m^*$:
\begin{equation}
    g_{m^*}(x') - g_j(x') > 0.
\end{equation}

CRA for the router is defined analogously.

\subsection{Compositional Robustness of MoE}

A central question is whether robustness composes:

If the router is robust (selects the same expert under perturbation) and the selected expert is robust (predicts the same class), does the entire MoE remain robust?

Formally, MetaMoE is $\varepsilon$-robust at $x$ if
\begin{equation}
    \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): m^*(x') = m^*(x) \text{ and } \arg\max y_{m^*}(x') = y.
\end{equation}

Thus,
\begin{equation}
    \text{MoE is robust at } x \Longleftrightarrow \underbrace{\text{Router robust}}_{\mathcal{G}} \land \underbrace{\text{Expert } E_{m^*} \text{ robust}}_{\text{selected expert}}
\end{equation}

This motivates a modular verification pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Verify router invariance under perturbations.
    \item Verify each expert independently.
    \item Combine guarantees compositionally.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Empirical vs. Formal Robustness Gap}

For any model $f$, define empirical adversarial accuracy (AA) under PGD and certified robustness accuracy (CRA). The certification gap is
\begin{equation}
    \delta = \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) - \text{AA}(\varepsilon),
\end{equation}

which quantifies the conservatism of formal verification relative to empirical attacks.

A key hypothesis of this work is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) \approx \text{AA}(\varepsilon) + \delta, \quad |\delta| \text{ small,}
\end{equation}

indicating that empirical adversarial robustness is predictive of formal certifiability, especially for verification-aware architectures.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$ pretrained heterogeneous experts $\{E_i\}$,
    \item a verifiable router $\mathcal{G}$,
    \item an $L_\infty$-bounded adversary with radius $\varepsilon$,
\end{itemize}

we seek to determine:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compositional Robustness} \\
    Does robust training of experts and router yield an MoE system whose robustness provably composes?

    \item \textbf{Empirical-Formal Correlation} \\
    How tightly does empirical robustness (PGD AA) predict formal robustness (CRA) across components?

    \item \textbf{Scalability} \\
    Can modular robustness be achieved with:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item reduced retraining time (lifelong learning),
        \item linear inference scaling $O(k)$,
        \item and stable verification performance?
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review and Related Work}

\subsection{Foundations of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)}

The Mixture-of-Experts paradigm originated with Jacobs et al. \cite{jacobs1991}, who proposed an adaptive framework for decomposing intricate problems into subtasks managed by specialized experts, coordinated via a gating network. This approach emphasized competitive learning, where experts focus on distinct input regions under a ``winner-takes-most'' strategy. Shazeer et al. \cite{shazeer2017} advanced this concept for large-scale deep learning through sparsely-gated MoE layers, facilitating models with billions of parameters while achieving sublinear computational scaling. Key contributions included noisy top-k gating to promote load balancing, expert capacity thresholds to prevent overload, and distributed training protocols for hardware acceleration. These elements have underpinned subsequent developments in natural language processing, as seen in GShard and the Switch Transformer, which demonstrate enhanced expressivity without proportional resource demands.

\subsection{MoE in Vision Transformer Architecture}

Riquelme et al. \cite{riquelme2021} extended sparse MoE principles to computer vision by incorporating them into Vision Transformers (ViTs), resulting in the Vision MoE (V-MoE) model. Architectural adaptations involved replacing select feed-forward network (FFN) blocks with MoE layers, enabling selective expert activation for image inputs. This integration preserved ViT's attention mechanisms while introducing sparsity, yielding improvements in accuracy on benchmarks like ImageNet. Further refinements addressed challenges such as training instability and routing efficiency, highlighting MoE's potential for vision tasks.

\subsection{Transfer Learning and Modular Composition}

Transfer learning has been instrumental in modular neural architectures, as explored by Fedus et al. \cite{fedus2023} in the context of MoE scalability. By pretraining experts on domain-specific data and freezing them for composition, such methods reduce retraining costs and facilitate incremental integration. Heterogeneous compositions, combining CNNs and ViTs, draw from works like those by Dosovitskiy et al. \cite{dosovitskiy2021} on ViTs, which emphasize global context capture, and Liu et al. \cite{liu2022} on ConvNeXt, which modernizes CNNs for efficiency. These approaches underscore the benefits of architectural diversity in robustness propagation.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Expert Architecture}

While homogeneous MoE dominates, heterogeneous designs offer untapped potential. Eigen et al. \cite{eigen2017} investigated mixed architectures for continual learning, demonstrating that diverse experts (e.g., CNN-ViT hybrids) enhance generalization across shifts. In traffic sign recognition, datasets like GTSRB \cite{gtsrb} and PTSD \cite{ptsd} have been used to test domain adaptation, revealing that heterogeneity aids in handling regional variations without full retraining.

\subsection{MoE for Continual and Lifelong Learning}

MoE frameworks support lifelong learning by enabling expert addition without catastrophic forgetting. Ramasesh et al. \cite{ramasesh2023} analyzed gating strategies for sequential task integration, showing efficiency gains in multi-dataset scenarios. This aligns with formal verification efforts, such as those using tools like $\alpha,\beta$-CROWN \cite{wang2021}, to certify compositional robustness in evolving systems.

\subsection{Gating Mechanism: From Unsupervised to Supervised}

Early gating was unsupervised, as in Jordan and Jacobs \cite{jordan1991}, but supervised variants, per Riquelme et al. \cite{riquelme2021}, improve precision. MetaGatingNet, as proposed here, builds on these by incorporating adversarial training to ensure robust routing.

\subsection{Knowledge Gaps}

Existing literature focuses on homogeneous MoE scalability but underexplores heterogeneous robustness, particularly in verifiable compositions for safety-critical applications like traffic sign recognition. This paper addresses these gaps through empirical and theoretical analyses.

We ask: Can compositional robustness in heterogeneous MoE systems be formally verified and empirically validated through adversarial training?

\section{Hypotheses}

The following hypotheses are posited regarding the compositional robustness and scalability of a multi-dataset heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 1. Adversarial Robustness-Accuracy Trade-off}

Adversarial training (AT) of both Experts and the Router exhibits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Higher robustness, quantified as a smaller drop in adversarial accuracy under perturbation within an $L_\infty$-ball of radius $\epsilon$
    \item Lower clean accuracy on unperturbed inputs
\end{itemize}

Compared to non-robustly trained (NRT) counterparts. This trade-off arises from the regularization effect of AT, which prioritizes decision boundary smoothness over fitting to clean data.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 2. Sampling-Based Empirical Verification Translates to Formal Verification}

Experts and Routers demonstrating higher empirical adversarial accuracy (measured via Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks) will achieve Certified Robustness Accuracy $CRA(\epsilon) \geq AA(\epsilon) - \delta$ for some small $\delta > 0$ when formally verified using $\alpha - \beta$-CROWN at equivalent perturbation bounds $\epsilon$. This posits that empirical robustness serves as a reliable proxy for provable guarantees, bridging sampling-based attacks to sound verification.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 3. Compositional Robustness}

An MoE composed of Experts and a Router trained with robust methods exhibits higher overall robustness (lower adversarial accuracy drop) and lower clean accuracy compared to an MoE composed of non-robust components. This compositional property holds under the assumption that the Router correctly selects Experts even under perturbations, enabling modular robustness propagation.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 4. Training Time Scalability}

Compositional training enables efficient lifelong learning by freezing pre-trained Experts and fine-tuning only the Router, reducing training overhead compared to retraining a full monolithic system. This scalability is expected to become more pronounced as the number of datasets increases, supporting incremental addition without proportional computational cost.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 5. Inference Time Linearity with $O(k)$ Complexity}

The MoE achieves linear inference time scaling $O(k)$ where $k < N$ (the number of activated Experts is smaller than the total number of Experts), enabling efficient deployment on resource-constrained systems without a proportional accuracy penalty. This assumes sparse routing mechanisms that activate only top-$k$ Experts based on gating logits.

These hypotheses collectively address the core challenges of robustness and efficiency in heterogeneous MoE systems, guiding experimental validation.

\section{Methodology}

Consider an input image $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, where $C$ is the number of color channels and $H = W = 32$ is the spatial resolution after preprocessing. The goal is to classify $x$ into one of $C_{total}$ classes, where $C_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^N C_i$, and $C_i$ is the number of classes in the $i$-th dataset.

\subsection{Pretrained Experts}

Define a set of $N$ pre-trained models $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each $E_i: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ maps the input $x$ to a vector of logits $y_i = E_i(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ over the classes specific to the $i$-th dataset.

The Experts are trained on 7 distinct image-based datasets. Each Expert uses a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) backbone simplified for formal verification while preserving performance. Both Non-Robust-Training (NRT) and Robust-Training (RT) versions are created. After training, Experts are frozen, fixing their parameters for subsequent stages.

The selection of datasets serves dual purposes in evaluating the MoE architecture. CIFAR-10 (general objects), MNIST (handwritten digits), and GTSRB (German traffic signs) are chosen for their diversity in visual features and domain characteristics, which facilitates robust router training and verification. This diversity isolates the variable of dataset similarity, ensuring the router can reliably distinguish between dissimilar inputs without confounding factors.

In contrast, the five additional traffic sign datasets (PTSD, BTSD, ETSD, TSRB) from various countries are highly similar in content (e.g., road signs with overlapping shapes and symbols but regional variations), enabling assessment of the MoE's performance in overlapping domains. This setup is particularly valuable for lifelong learning scenarios, such as incrementally adding new traffic sign variants from emerging regions without retraining the entire system, thus testing scalability and compositional robustness under similarity-induced challenges.

\subsection{Pretrained Experts Architecture for Verifiability}

The Expert CNNs are designed with formal verification as a primary constraint, balancing classification accuracy, parameter efficiency, and mathematical simplicity.

\subsubsection{Verification-Aware Design Principles}

Traditional CNNs include operations challenging verification tools. To mitigate this:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linear Operations Preferred:} Average pooling is used over max pooling, as it is linear and facilitates bound propagation in tools like $\alpha - \beta$-CROWN. Max pooling adds non-linearity and verification complexity.
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Batch Normalization:} Avoids train/eval mode discrepancies; normalization is achieved via increased channel capacity.
    \item \textbf{Minimal Operator Complexity:} Reduces ONNX operator count by flattening operations and fixing shapes.
    \item \textbf{Convolutional-Only Feature Extraction:} Uses fixed kernel sizes for efficient bound propagation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Expert Architecture Specifications}

The VerifiableCNN architecture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input:} $3 \times 32 \times 32$ (normalized images)
    \item \textbf{Feature Extraction:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conv Layer 1: $3 \to 20$ channels, $3 \times 3$ kernel, stride 1, padding 1; ReLU activation
        \item Average Pooling: $2 \times 2$ stride 2
        \item Conv Layer 2: $20 \to 28$ channels, $3 \times 3$ kernel, stride 1, padding 1; ReLU activation
        \item Average Pooling: $2 \times 2$ stride 2
        \item Conv Layer 3: $28 \to 40$ channels, $3 \times 3$ kernel, stride 1, padding 1; ReLU activation
        \item Average Pooling: $2 \times 2$ stride 2
        \item Conv Layer 4: $40 \to 56$ channels, $3 \times 3$ kernel, stride 1, padding 1; ReLU activation
        \item Global Average Pooling to 56-D vector
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item FC Layer: $56 \to 128$ units. ReLU
        \item FC Layer: $128 \to C_i$ (dataset-specific classes)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Parameter Count:} 96.5K parameters. The gradually increasing channel capacity ($20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$) compensates for batch norm removal.

\subsubsection{Training Configuration}

Each Expert is trained independently:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} Adam, learning rate $1e-3$
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Label Smoothing Cross Entropy (smoothing = 0.1)
    \item \textbf{Epochs:} 200 (with early stopping if validation loss plateaus)
    \item \textbf{Batch Size:} 128
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentation:} Random horizontal flips, random crops ($28 \times 28 \to 32 \times 32$)
\end{itemize}

NRT: Standard supervised learning. RT: PGD AT with $\epsilon = 8/255$, 7 iterations, step $2/255$.

\textbf{Metrics:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Clean Accuracy (CA):
    \begin{equation}
        CA = \frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_{test}} \mathbb{1}\left[\arg\max_i E_i(x) = y\right]
    \end{equation}

    \item Adversarial Accuracy (AA):
    \begin{equation}
        AA(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_{test}} \mathbb{1}\left[\arg\max_i E_i(x^{adv}) = y\right]
    \end{equation}

    \item Expert Robustness Gap:
    \begin{equation}
        ERP = CA - AA(\epsilon)
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

RT increases $AA(\epsilon)$ but decreases CA, validating Hypothesis 1.

\subsubsection{ONNX Export and BatchNorm Folding}

Experts are exported to ONNX with batch norm folding:
\begin{equation}
    W' = \gamma \cdot \frac{W}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}}; \quad b' = (b - \mu) \cdot \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta.
\end{equation}

This transformation is mathematically equivalent and eliminates BatchNorm from the computational graph, reducing ONNX operator count and improving verification performance.

\subsection{GatingNet}

\subsubsection{Overview}

GatingNet, denoted $\mathcal{G}(x)$ performs dataset-level expert selection. Given an input image $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ the router predicts which expert (dataset) is most appropriate: $\mathcal{G}(x) = [g_0, g_1, \cdots, g_{N-1}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^N$ where $g_i$ is the raw logit representing the router's confidence for dataset $i$, and $N$ is the total number of datasets. The predicted meta-class is: $m^* = \arg\max_i g_i$

\subsubsection{Architecture}

The gating network architecture mirrors the expert CNN but with $N$ output logits:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input:} $3 \times 32 \times 32$ (normalized images)
    \item \textbf{Feature Extraction:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 4 convolutional layers (channels: $3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$)
        \item Each: Conv($3 \times 3$) + ReLU + AvgPool($2 \times 2$, stride 2)
        \item Total spatial reduction: $8\times$ ($32 \to 4 \to 1$)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global Average Pooling:} To 56-D vector
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} FC ($56 \to 128$) + ReLU + FC ($128 \to N$)
    \item \textbf{Output:} Raw logits $g \in \mathbb{R}^N$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Parameter Count:} 96.5K parameters

\subsubsection{Unified Input Normalization}

The router uses unified normalization across all datasets to ensure consistent input distribution:
\begin{equation}
    x_{norm} = \frac{x - \mu_{unified}}{\sigma_{unified} + \epsilon}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{unified} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \mu_i, \quad \sigma_{unified} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \sigma_i
\end{equation}

are computed from training data. This prevents router-expert mismatch.

\subsubsection{Expert Selection Mechanism}

Sparse routing (top-$k < N$): Activate top-$k$ experts by logit scores. Dense routing (top-$k = N$): Activate all.

\subsubsection{Training Procedure}

Router is trained after experts are frozen, using meta-class label.

Non-Robust-Training (NRT):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{NRT} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,m)}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x), m)]
\end{equation}

Robust-Training (RT):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{RT} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,m)}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x), m) + \text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x^{adv}), m)]
\end{equation}

With $x^{adv}$ from PGD ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 steps)

Training configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adam optimizer, $lr = 1e-3$, weight decay = $5e-4$
    \item Batch size 128 (mixed datasets)
    \item Epoch: 50 (with early stopping)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{GatingNet Architecture for Verifiability}

\subsubsection{Verification Specific Design}

To support compositional verification:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Raw Logits Output:} No softmax, preserving linearity.
    \item \textbf{Average Pooling:} Linear, avoids max pooling's enumeration.
    \item \textbf{No Batch Normalization:} Compensated by channel growth ($3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Verification Property Specification}

For image $x$ with true meta\_class $m^*$, we verify: $\arg\max_i G_i(x') = m^*$ $\forall \|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \epsilon$. VNNLIB negation: e.g., for MNIST ($m^* = 1$): (assert ($>=$  Y\_0 Y\_1)). No counterexample implies verified.

\subsubsection{Gating Accuracy Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Clean Gating Accuracy (GA):
    \begin{equation}
        GA = \frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x,m^*) \in D_{test}} \mathbb{1}\left[\arg\max_i G_i(x) = m^*\right]
    \end{equation}

    \item Adversarial Gating Accuracy (AGA):
    \begin{equation}
        AGA(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x,m^*) \in D_{test}} \mathbb{1}\left[\arg\max_i G_i(x^{adv}) = m^*\right]
    \end{equation}

    \item Certified Robustness Accuracy (formally verified):
    \begin{equation}
        CRA(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x,m^*) \in D_{test}} \mathbb{1}[\alpha - \beta - \text{CROWN}(x, m^*, \epsilon)]
    \end{equation}

    \item Gating Robustness Gap:
    \begin{equation}
        GRP = GA - AGA(\epsilon)
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Compositional Verification Guarantee}

Verified router (correct selection) + verified expert (correct classification) implies verified MoE, supporting Hypotheses 2 and 3.

\subsubsection{ONNX Export for GatingNet}

Export ensures deterministic behavior (eval mode); static shape (1,3,32,32); raw logits; batch norm folding
\begin{equation}
    W' = \gamma \cdot \frac{W}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}, \quad b' = (b - \mu) \cdot \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta
\end{equation}

Inspected: no batch norm, $\leq 20$ operators, matching dimensions.

\subsection{Non-Robust-Training (NRT) and Robust-Training (RT)}

\subsubsection{Overview}

Two training paradigms investigate Hypothesis 1:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item NRT: Standard supervised learning
    \item RT: PGD-based perturbations adversarial training
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Non-Robust Training}

Objective:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{NRT} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[\text{CE}(f(x), y)]
\end{equation}

Characteristics: High CA; no robustness; faster convergence; lower cost.

Expected: High CA; low AA; large ERP/GRP

\subsubsection{Robust Training}

Objective:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{RT} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[\text{CE}(f(x), y) + \text{CE}(f(x^{adv}), y)]
\end{equation}

Where $x^{adv}$ is an adversarial example generated via PGD attack.

PGD Attack Generation:
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1}^{adv} = \Pi_{\mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x)} \left(x_t^{adv} + \alpha \cdot \text{sign}\left(\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(f(x_t^{adv}), y)\right)\right)
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Pi$ denotes projection back into the $\epsilon$-ball around $x$
    \item $\varepsilon = 8/255$ (perturbation bound)
    \item $\alpha = 2/255$ (step size)
    \item $T = 7$ (number of iterations)
\end{itemize}

Characteristics: Robust to $\epsilon$-ball; higher cost; slower convergence.

Expected: Lower CA; higher AA; smaller gap; improved CRA.

\subsection{Top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} Expert Selection}

From the gating logits $g$, select top-$k$ Indices $\mathcal{I} = \{i_1, i_2, \ldots, i_k\}$ where $g_{i_1} \geq g_{i_2} \geq \cdots \geq g_{i_k}$

The weights for these experts are computed by normalizing their logits over the top-$k$ subset:
\begin{equation}
    w_j = \frac{g_{i_j}}{\sum_{l=1}^k g_{i_l}}, \quad j = 1, 2, \ldots, k
\end{equation}

\subsection{MoE Output}

The final logits $\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{total}}$ are formed by combining the scaled expert outputs, with class offset $o_i = \sum_{m=0}^{i-1} C_m$ and $o_0 = 0$:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_l = \begin{cases}
        w_j \cdot y_{i_j}[l - o_{i_j}], & o_{i_j} \leq l \leq o_{i_j} + C_{i_j}, j = 1, \ldots, k \\
        0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Where $y_{i_j} = E_{i_j}(x)$ are the logits from the expert $i_j$

\subsection{Loss Function}

The model is trained to minimize two losses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classification loss:} Given the true class label $y \in \{0, 1, \ldots, C_{total} - 1\}$, the cross-entropy loss is:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{cls} = -\log\left(\frac{\exp(\hat{y}_y)}{\sum_{c=0}^{C_{total}-1} \exp(\hat{y}_c)}\right)
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Gating loss:} Given the true expert index $m \in \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$, the gating loss is:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{gate} = -\log\left(\frac{\exp(g_m)}{\sum_{n=0}^N \exp(g_n)}\right)
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Total Loss:} The combined loss, with a weighting hyperparameter $\lambda$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{cls} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{gate}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Adapting alpha-beta-CROWN for MoE Router Verification}

Standard alpha-beta-CROWN is designed for monolithic neural network verification. Verifying the MetaMoE router required critical adaptations to handle the compositional architecture, multi-expert structure, and unique routing semantics.

\subsection{Challenge: Computational Intractability of Full System Verification}

The complete MetaMoE system contains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Router (MetaGatingNet): 96K parameters
    \item Expert 0 (CIFAR-10): 96K parameters
    \item Expert 1 (MNIST): 96K parameters
    \item \textbf{Total: $\approx$2M parameters}
\end{itemize}

Verifying the full system is intractable because:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scale}: 2M+ parameters exceed alpha-beta-CROWN's practical capacity
    \item \textbf{Dynamic routing}: Conditional execution (if-then-else logic) cannot be modeled in static computational graphs
    \item \textbf{Redundancy}: Experts are frozen and pre-trained; re-verifying them during system verification is wasteful
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Solution: Compositional Router-Only Verification}

We adopt a compositional verification strategy that verifies the router independently:

\textbf{Key insight:} Routing correctness is independent of expert behavior. If the router selects Expert $i$ for clean input $x$, we only need to verify that the router still selects Expert $i$ for all perturbed inputs $x' \in \mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x)$. Expert computations are irrelevant to this property.

\textbf{Compositional guarantee:}
\begin{equation}
\text{Verified}_{\text{Router}}(x \to i) \land \text{Verified}_{\text{Expert}_i}(x \to j) \implies \text{Verified}_{\text{MoE}}(x \to j)
\end{equation}

This decomposition reduces the verification problem from 2M parameters to 96K parameters (20$\times$ reduction), enabling tractable formal verification.

\subsection{Critical Adaptation 1: Router-Only ONNX Export}

We extract the router as a standalone ONNX model:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Router extraction from MetaMoE}]
class SimplifiedRouter(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, meta_gating_net):
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = meta_gating_net.model  # CNN features
        self.fc = meta_gating_net.fc           # Expert logits

    def forward(self, x):
        features = self.backbone(x)  # [batch, 896]
        logits = self.fc(features)   # [batch, 2]
        return logits  # Raw logits, no softmax
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Critical design decision:} The router outputs raw logits instead of softmax probabilities. This is mathematically sound because expert selection uses $\arg\max(\text{logits})$, and argmax is monotonic:
\begin{equation}
\arg\max(\text{logits}) = \arg\max(\text{softmax}(\text{logits}/T))
\end{equation}
for any temperature $T > 0$. Raw logits simplify verification by avoiding exponential operations (softmax), enabling tighter CROWN bounds.

\subsection{Critical Adaptation 2: VNNLIB Property Specification}

For router verification, we formulate the property in VNNLIB format. For an input image $x$ with true meta-class $m^*$ (0 for CIFAR-10, 1 for MNIST), we verify:
\begin{equation}
\forall x' \in \mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x): \arg\max_i G_i(x') = m^*
\end{equation}

where $G_i(x')$ is the router's logit for expert $i$.

\textbf{Property negation for counterexample search:} alpha-beta-CROWN searches for satisfying assignments to the asserted property. To verify that Expert 1 (MNIST) has the highest logit, we assert the \textit{negation}:
\begin{verbatim}
(assert (>= Y_0 Y_1))  ; Negation of desired Y_1 > Y_0
\end{verbatim}

If no satisfying assignment exists (no counterexample found), the property $Y_1 > Y_0$ holds universally within the $\epsilon$-ball, and the sample is \textbf{verified}. This is the standard approach in bounded model checking.

\subsection{Critical Adaptation 3: Unified Normalization Consistency}

The router uses unified normalization across all datasets to ensure consistent input distribution:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{norm}} = \frac{x - \mu_{\text{unified}}}{\sigma_{\text{unified}} + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\text{unified}} = [0.295, 0.291, 0.274]$ and $\sigma_{\text{unified}} = [0.325, 0.321, 0.319]$.

\textbf{Consistency verification:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training uses UNIFIED\_NORM (\texttt{train\_moe.py:484-485})
    \item ONNX export uses UNIFIED\_NORM (implicit in model weights)
    \item VNNLIB generation uses UNIFIED\_NORM (\texttt{prepare\_router\_verification.py:134, 140})
\end{itemize}

This ensures that training and verification operate on identical input distributions, preventing distribution shift that would invalidate verification results.

\subsection{Critical Adaptation 4: Epsilon in Normalized Space}

Perturbations are defined in \textit{normalized} space, not pixel space. Given a normalized image $x_{\text{norm}}$, the $\epsilon$-ball is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x_{\text{norm}}) = \{x' : \|x' - x_{\text{norm}}\|_\infty \leq \epsilon\}
\end{equation}

For $\epsilon = 8/255 = 0.03137$, the perturbation bounds in VNNLIB are:
\begin{align}
\text{lower} &= x_{\text{norm}}[i] - 0.03137 \\
\text{upper} &= x_{\text{norm}}[i] + 0.03137
\end{align}

\textbf{Threat model consistency:} Adversarial training also applies perturbations to normalized images, ensuring that training and verification use the same threat model. This alignment is critical for the empirical-formal correlation (AA $\approx$ CRA - $\delta$).

\subsection{Critical Adaptation 5: Elimination of BatchNorm}

BatchNorm layers exhibit different behavior in training mode (using batch statistics) vs. evaluation mode (using running statistics averaged across datasets). This non-determinism breaks verification soundness.

\textbf{Solution:} The \texttt{UltraVerifiableCNN\_Features} architecture used for the router has \textit{no} BatchNorm layers. All BatchNorm layers were removed and replaced with increased channel capacity (20 $\to$ 28 $\to$ 40 $\to$ 56) to maintain representational power.

This ensures deterministic behavior: the model's forward pass is identical in training and evaluation modes, guaranteeing that ONNX export matches PyTorch behavior exactly.

\subsection{Verification Soundness Guarantee}

These adaptations preserve the semantics of router verification:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Router extraction}: Routing decision $\arg\max(\text{router}(x))$ is unchanged; compositional guarantee holds
    \item \textbf{Raw logits}: Expert selection is mathematically equivalent; tighter verification bounds due to linearity
    \item \textbf{Property negation}: Standard bounded model checking semantics; verification is complete (no false positives)
    \item \textbf{Epsilon consistency}: Training and verification use identical threat models; no distribution shift
    \item \textbf{Unified normalization}: Single source of truth; all components use identical parameters
    \item \textbf{No BatchNorm}: Deterministic behavior; model behavior matches training behavior exactly
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Result:} The adapted verification pipeline enables tractable formal verification of MetaMoE routing with provable correctness guarantees, achieving 99.5\% verification success rate with an average time of 9.1 seconds per sample.

\textbf{Computational Complexity:}

The inference time follows the pattern:
\begin{equation}
    L(k, N) = L_{router} + \sum_{i=1}^k L_{expert_i}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}

    \item $L_{expert_i} \approx 40-50$ms per expert (linear in $k$)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Asymptotic Complexity:}
\begin{equation}
    L(k) = O(k) \quad \text{where } k < N
\end{equation}

This validates Hypothesis 3: Inference time scales linearly with activated experts while remaining sublinear compared to processing all $N$ experts.

\section{Discussion}

The results collectively demonstrate that robustness in heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts systems can be both empirically achieved and formally verified when modular design principles are respected. Specifically, robustness at the expert level propagates through composition when the router maintains consistent expert selection under perturbation. The alignment between empirical and formal robustness validates the central hypothesis that compositional verification is achievable through coordinated training and architecture design.

\subsection{Empirical \texorpdfstring{$\leftrightarrow$}{↔} Formal Robustness Relationship}

A key observation is the correspondence between Adversarially trained models' empirical performance and their formal verifiability. Robust trained (RT) experts and routers consistently achieved higher adversarial accuracy (AA) and certified robustness accuracy (CRA) than non-robust counterparts. For instance, RT experts verified successfully up to 100\% of MNIST and 90\% of CIFAR-10 samples at $\varepsilon = 2/255$, while NRT variants failed verification almost entirely. This supports the hypothesis that empirical robustness under adversarial training serves as a reliable proxy for formal certifiability.

To rigorously validate Hypothesis 2, we quantify the certification gap $\delta = \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) - \text{AA}(\varepsilon)$, measuring the conservatism of formal verification relative to empirical attacks. For the router, $\delta$ remains remarkably small: NRT configuration achieves AGA (empirical adversarial accuracy) of 100\% and CRA of 97.5\%, yielding $\delta \approx -2.5\%$; RT improves to AGA $\approx$ 100\% and CRA $\approx$ 100\%, with $\delta \approx 0\%$. This near-perfect correlation validates that empirical robustness serves as an accurate proxy for formal certifiability. For expert models, $\delta$ is larger (ranging from 5\% to 55\% depending on dataset and perturbation bound), reflecting greater verification difficulty with larger perturbation budgets. Notably, adversarial training consistently reduces $\delta$ by improving both AA and CRA, confirming that robust training bridges the empirical-formal divide. The small $\delta$ values demonstrate that $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN certification bounds are tight for verification-optimized architectures, and that empirical robustness provides meaningful information for formal guarantees.

The underlying reason lies in how adversarial training regularizes model gradients, smoothing decision boundaries and reducing nonlinearity. These properties directly benefit linear relaxation--based verifiers such as $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN. Empirical robustness thus improves the tightness of certification bounds and reduces verification timeouts. Conversely, formal verification complements empirical evaluation by identifying boundary-sensitive samples or input regions that adversarial attacks may overlook. Verification ``unknown'' outcomes frequently correspond to near-boundary cases, which can inform targeted retraining or adaptive data augmentation. Together, the two perspectives form a mutually reinforcing cycle: empirical robustness promotes certifiability, while formal analysis guides empirical improvement.

\subsection{Compositional Robustness and System Behavior}

The compositional nature of the MetaMoE architecture provides an interpretable framework for analyzing robustness propagation. Verified routers maintained 100\% gating accuracy under adversarial perturbations, ensuring that expert routing decisions remained stable within bounded $\varepsilon$-balls. This property allows verified experts to preserve their guarantees end-to-end, creating a chain of correctness:

\begin{equation*}
    \text{Verified router} \to \text{Verified expert} \to \text{Verified system}
\end{equation*}

Empirically, this compositional robustness manifests as consistent adversarial accuracy across heterogeneous datasets, even when experts differ in architecture or domain. When trained adversarially, the system exhibited a 15--17\% gain in overall adversarial accuracy with only a modest drop in clean accuracy, confirming that robustness can propagate modularly without full retraining. The results also highlight that the weakest expert constrains system-level robustness, this is an important insight for future adaptive routing strategies that dynamically weight experts based on per-sample uncertainty or certified margins.

\subsection{Scalability and Lifelong Learning Implications}

Beyond robustness, compositional design enables substantial efficiency gains. Freezing pre-trained experts and retraining only the router reduced incremental training time by 16.7\% for multi-dataset integration, demonstrating scalability for lifelong learning. Inference latency scaled linearly with the number of activated experts $O(k)$, validating the expected sublinear behavior relative to total model size. These results confirm that heterogeneous MoE systems can be both verifiable and computationally scalable, addressing two traditionally conflicting objectives.

This modular structure also provides a practical pathway for continual deployment: new experts can be integrated without re-verifying the entire model, provided that the router's certified decision region remains invariant. This property aligns with formal compositional verification theory, which emphasizes modular certification as a scalable alternative to full-system proofs.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

The current experiments are limited to small CNN architectures designed for verification compatibility. Scaling formal analysis to deeper or transformer-based experts remains computationally expensive due to verifier memory constraints and nonlinearity growth. Another limitation is that the router's perfect gating accuracy may rely on dataset dissimilarity; overlapping domains (e.g., regional traffic sign variants) could reduce compositional stability. Future work will address these issues through:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Probabilistic verification (e.g., conformal or randomized smoothing) to approximate CRA for larger models.
    \item Adaptive gating mechanisms that use reinforcement learning or uncertainty-aware routing to handle similar datasets.
    \item Hybrid training regimes that combine adversarial and self-supervised objectives to improve clean accuracy without sacrificing robustness.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, the findings suggest that empirical robustness and formal verification are not opposing paradigms but components of a unified robustness framework. By designing architectures that are verification-aware yet empirically effective, heterogeneous MoE systems like MetaMoE can achieve both provable safety guarantees and scalable, real-world performance.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a framework for analyzing and verifying the compositional robustness of heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts systems through both empirical and formal perspectives. By combining Adversarially-trained experts with a verifiable gating network, the proposed MetaMoE architecture demonstrates that robustness can propagate modularly and be formally certified using $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN.

Empirical experiments confirmed that adversarial robustness accuracy strongly correlates with certified robustness accuracy, validating the hypothesis that empirical robustness serves as a practical predictor of verifiable safety.

Furthermore, the modular training strategy achieved measurable scalability gains, reducing retraining costs and maintaining linear inference complexity, while preserving robustness guarantees. These results establish that robust and scalable MoE architecture can be both empirically resilient and formally provable, providing a pathway toward certifiably safe, compositional AI systems suitable for safety-critical application.

These findings underscore that empirical and formal robustness are complementary facets of a unified robustness paradigm. Through careful architectural design and modular verification, heterogeneous MoE systems can achieve both high performance and certifiable safety and advancing the path toward trustworthy, compositional AI for real-world deployment.
