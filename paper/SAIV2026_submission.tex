\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures offer modularity and scalability, yet their robustness and certifiability, especially in heterogeneous settings, remain poorly understood. This work presents \textbf{MetaMoE}, a heterogeneous MoE framework designed for both adversarial robustness and formal verifiability. We study how robustness propagates compositionally across experts and a routing network, and establish when system-level verification can be derived from component-level verification.

We show that, firstly, for visually dissimilar datasets, router training has negligible effect and adversarially trained experts are the main determiner for system robustness; secondly, given an architecture, empirical adversarial accuracy correlates with certified robustness accuracy (CRA), with robust training significantly improving verifiability; and thirdly, when the router maintains invariant expert selection within an adversarial perturbation region, robustness of the routed expert will compose to the robustness of the full MoE system, enabling scalable modular verification.

Experiments on heterogeneous datasets demonstrate that robust experts improve adversarial accuracy by up to 13.1 percentage points and enable higher formal certifiability, while verified routers achieve 100\% certified routing accuracy at practical threat models for dissimilar datasets. The results provide a unified empirical--formal perspective on heterogeneous MoE robustness and outline a principled path toward scalable, verifiable modular AI systems.

\keywords{Mixture-of-experts \and Adversarial Robustness \and Formal Verification \and Compositional Verification \and Neural Network Verification \and Safety-Critical AI}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) perform well on vision and language tasks but remain vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations~\cite{goodfellow2015,madry2018}. This unreliability is hard to justify in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, robotics, and medical imaging. In parallel, Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures~\cite{jacobs1991,shazeer2017} have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for scaling models through modular computation and conditional nodes activation. While large homogeneous MoE models have demonstrated impressive scalability, their robustness properties, particularly in heterogeneous settings where domain-specific experts must cooperate, are not well studied.

Formal verification tools, such as $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~\cite{wang2021}, enable provable robustness guarantees for neural networks. However, verifying full MoE systems still remains challenging due to their size, sparsity, and especially dynamic routing behavior. A key open question is whether robustness \emph{composes}: can expert-level robustness and router-level robustness be combined to yield robustness guarantees for the full MoE? If so, heterogeneous MoE systems could be trained, extended, and certified modularly.

This paper introduces \textbf{MetaMoE}, a verification-aware heterogeneous MoE architecture that combines verifiable experts with a verifiable routing network, intermixing adversarially and non-adversarially trained experts and router. We investigate how empirical adversarial robustness (PGD accuracy) relates to certified robustness (formal bounds), and establish conditions under which robustness propagates from components to the entire system. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Formalize a \emph{compositional robustness proposition}: for hard-routed MoE, where only one expert is activated at any given time, if the router maintains invariant expert selection within an $\ell_\infty$ ball and the selected expert is robust, then the full MoE is robust. We prove this sufficient condition and validate it~empirically.
    \item Identify an association between empirical adversarial accuracy and certified robustness within domain/architecture regimes, and demonstrating that robust training is a prerequisite for verifiability of large and complex neural network architectures.
    \item For visually dissimilar domains, we demonstrate that expert robustness dominates system behavior, while router adversarial training has negligible impact.
    \item We show that compositional verification significantly reduces verification temporal and spatial cost, enabling scalable modular certification and efficient incremental expert integration.
\end{itemize}

Together, these findings establish a unified empirical and formal understanding of robustness in heterogeneous MoE systems and provide a foundation for scalable, verifiable modular AI.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ denote an input image (normalized, $H = W = 32$) with target class $y \in \{1, \ldots, C_{total}\}$ where $C_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i$, and $C_i$ is the number of classes in dataset $i$. A heterogeneous MoE consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experts:} $E_i: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ producing logits $y_i = E_i(x)$ for domain $i$
    \item \textbf{Router:} $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^N$ producing routing logits $g = \mathcal{G}(x)$
\end{enumerate}

The router selects expert $m^* = \arg\max_i g_i$. For top\nobreakdash-$k$ routing, let $\mathcal{I}_k(g) = \{i_1, \ldots, i_k\}$ be the selected expert indices with normalized weights $w_j = g_{i_j} / \sum_{\ell=1}^k g_{i_\ell}$. The MoE output is $\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{total}}}$ with class offsets $o_i = \sum_{m<i} C_m$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_c = \begin{cases}
        w_j \cdot y_{i_j}[c - o_{i_j}], & \text{if } c \in \{o_{i_j}, \ldots, o_{i_j} + C_{i_j} - 1\} \\
        0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adversarial Threat Model and Robustness}

We consider $L_\infty$-bounded perturbations $\delta$ with $\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon$, producing adversarial examples $x' = x + \delta$ in the $\varepsilon$-ball $B_\varepsilon(x)$. Robustness requires:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expert $E_i$:} $\arg\max y_i(x') = y \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$
    \item \textbf{Router $\mathcal{G}$:} $m^*(x') = m^*(x) \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, or equivalently $g_{m^*}(x') - g_j(x') > 0$ for all $j \neq m^*$
    \item \textbf{MoE:} $\arg\max_c \hat{y}_c(x') = y \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$
\end{itemize}

Certified robustness accuracy (CRA) measures the fraction of samples verified robust:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D} \mathbbm{1}[\text{Verify}(f, x, \varepsilon)]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Compositional Robustness}

We state the central compositional property for hard routing ($k=1$, argmax selection) for experts with different classes:

\begin{definition}[Router Invariance]
Router $\mathcal{G}$ is \emph{invariant} at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ if $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Compositional Robustness -- Sufficient Condition]
\label{prop:compositional}
Consider a hard-routed MoE ($k{=}1$) with router $\mathcal{G}$ and experts $\{E_i\}$. Let $m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$ denote the selected expert for input~$x$. If:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] $\mathcal{G}$ is invariant at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ (router selects $m^*$ throughout), and
    \item[(ii)] $E_{m^*}$ is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ (i.e., $\arg\max_c E_{m^*,c}(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$),
\end{enumerate}
then the MoE system is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under hard routing, $\hat{y}_c(x') = E_{m^*(x'),c'}(x')$ for the appropriate class offset. By assumption~(i), $m^*(x') = m^*$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, so the same expert is evaluated. By assumption~(ii), the selected expert preserves the correct classification. Hence $\arg\max_c \hat{y}_c(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. \qed
\end{proof}

\textbf{Converse:} The converse does not hold in general: if multiple candidate experts share overlapping classes and agree on the same output, the MoE may produce the correct output even if the router switches between experts within $B_\varepsilon(x)$. However, when each expert covers a \emph{disjoint} set of classes, as in our setting, the converse does hold. Under hard routing, only the selected expert produces output; if the router switches to expert $m' \neq m^*$, the output lies in $m'$'s class range, which does not contain the true label $y$, so the MoE necessarily mis-routed or mis-classified. Therefore, with disjoint expert classes, Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} provides a necessary and sufficient condition: the MoE is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ if and only if both router invariance and expert robustness hold. For soft or top-$k$ routing ($k > 1$), the sufficient condition requires invariance of the full top-$k$ set and robustness of all selected experts, which we do not address here. Our experiments use $k=1$.

MoE's robustness, in our setting, decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MoE robust at } x \Longleftrightarrow \text{Router robust} \land \text{Expert } E_{m^*} \text{ robust}
\end{equation}

We also examine the case where experts handle visually similar datasets: specifically, GTSRB and PTSD (both traffic sign recognition tasks with 43 classes each), where the disjoint-class assumption still holds but the router faces a harder discrimination problem. We report these results in Appendix~\ref{app:similarity}.

This decomposition enables modular verification: verify router invariance and expert robustness independently, then compose guarantees via Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}.

\subsection{Empirical--Formal Gap}

The certification gap $\Delta = \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) - \text{AA}(\varepsilon)$ quantifies the divergence between formal verification and empirical adversarial accuracy (AA) under PGD. Note that CRA and AA may measure different properties (e.g., router invariance vs.\ end-to-end classification); see the Metric Alignment discussion in Section~7.1. We hypothesize that within a given domain and architecture, higher AA monotonically associates with higher CRA, with robust training as a prerequisite for and predictive of verifiability.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given $N$ pretrained experts $\{E_i\}$, router $\mathcal{G}$, and $L_\infty$ adversary with radius $\varepsilon$, we investigate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compositional Robustness:} Does robust training of components yield provably robust MoE composition?
    \item \textbf{Empirical--Formal Correlation:} Does PGD adversarial accuracy predict certified robustness accuracy?
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} Can modular robustness achieve reduced retraining time, linear inference $O(k)$, and stable verification?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

The MoE paradigm~\cite{jacobs1991} decomposes problems into specialized subtasks via a gating network. Shazeer et al.~\cite{shazeer2017} scaled MoE through sparse gating, and Riquelme et al.~\cite{riquelme2021} extended it to vision (V-MoE). Fedus et al.~\cite{fedus2022} showed that freezing pretrained experts reduces retraining cost, which is critical for lifelong learning. Recent large-scale MoE systems, including DeepSeekMoE~\cite{dai2024}, Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024}, and DeepSeek-V3~\cite{deepseek2024}, demonstrate the scalability of sparse routing to hundreds of billions of parameters. MoE frameworks also mitigate catastrophic forgetting~\cite{ramasesh2022}, with gating evolving from unsupervised~\cite{jordan1994} to supervised~\cite{riquelme2021} approaches, and modular deep learning~\cite{pfeiffer2023} providing a broader framework for composable model design.

\textbf{Adversarial Robustness of MoE.} Despite scaling advances, the robustness of MoE systems remains understudied. Puigcerver et al.~\cite{puigcerver2022} showed that MoE models can be more robust than dense counterparts under certain conditions, while Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025moe} found that expert networks are more susceptible to adversarial attacks. Pavlitska et al.~\cite{pavlitska2025robust} studied the effect of adversarial training on CNNs with sparse MoE layers. However, none of these works address formal verification of MoE systems or compositional robustness guarantees.

\textbf{Neural Network Verification.} Verifying neural network robustness is NP-complete in general~\cite{katz2017}. $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~\cite{wang2021} provides state-of-the-art complete verification via bound propagation, winning VNN-COMP 2021--2025~\cite{kaulen2025}. Certified training approaches such as CROWN-IBP~\cite{zhang2020} and randomized smoothing~\cite{cohen2019} produce models with provable guarantees. PGD-based adversarial training~\cite{madry2018} provides empirical robustness that we show correlates with formal certifiability. However, existing verification work addresses monolithic models, not compositional architectures.

\textbf{Compositional Verification.} Assume-guarantee reasoning~\cite{henzinger1998} enables modular verification in classical systems. Zhou and Tripakis~\cite{zhou2024compositional} recently applied compositional inductive invariants to neural network controlled systems. Our work extends this principle to MoE architectures, where router invariance and expert robustness jointly compose to yield system-level~guarantees.

\textbf{Research Gap.} Prior literature studies MoE scalability~\cite{dai2024,deepseek2024}, MoE's adversarial robustness~\cite{puigcerver2022,zhang2025moe}, and monolithic verification~\cite{wang2021}, but does not address heterogeneous robustness or verifiable compositions. This paper bridges these gaps by combining adversarial training with formal verification to investigate: \textit{Can compositional robustness in heterogeneous MoE systems be formally verified and empirically validated?}

\section{Hypotheses}

The following hypotheses are posited regarding the compositional robustness and scalability of a multi-dataset heterogeneous MoE architecture.

\noindent\textbf{H1 (Adversarial Robustness--Accuracy Trade-off).}
Adversarial training (AT) of both experts and the router exhibits higher robustness (smaller drop in adversarial accuracy under $L_\infty$ perturbation of radius $\varepsilon$) but lower clean accuracy on unperturbed inputs, compared to non-robustly trained (NRT) counterparts. This trade-off arises from the regularization effect of AT, which prioritizes decision boundary smoothness over fitting to clean data.

\noindent\textbf{H2 (Empirical Verification Translates to Formal Verification).}
Within a given domain and architecture, models with higher empirical adversarial accuracy (via PGD attacks) will achieve higher certified robustness accuracy when formally verified using $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN. Furthermore, robust training is a prerequisite: non-robustly trained models on complex domains will be significantly more expensive to verify, temporally and spatially. This creates a bridge between empirical-based verification and theoretical-based verification.

\noindent\textbf{H3 (Compositional Robustness).}
An MoE composed of experts and a router trained with robust methods exhibits higher overall robustness and lower clean accuracy compared to an MoE composed of non-robust components. This compositional property holds under the assumption that the router correctly selects experts even under perturbations.

\noindent\textbf{H4 (Training Time Scalability).}
Freezing pretrained experts and training only the router enables efficient incremental learning. As the number of experts grows, this compositional strategy reduces retraining cost relative to monolithic models.

\noindent\textbf{H5 (Linear Inference Scaling).}
The MoE inference cost scales linearly with the number of activated experts $k < N$, i.e., $O(k)$, since only routed experts are evaluated.

These hypotheses collectively address the core challenges of robustness and efficiency in heterogeneous MoE systems, guiding experiment setup decisions.

\section{Methodology}

Given input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32}$ and $N$ datasets with $C_i$ classes each, we train $N$ frozen experts $E_i: \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ and a router $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32} \to \mathbb{R}^N$ to select appropriate experts. We evaluate on 7 datasets: CIFAR-10, MNIST, GTSRB (diverse domains~\cite{gtsrb}) and PTSD, BTSD, ETSD, TSRB (similar traffic sign domains for lifelong learning).

\subsection{Verification-Aware CNN Architecture}

Both experts and router use VerifiableCNN (96.5K parameters) designed for $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN compatibility: (1) average pooling instead of max pooling for linearity; (2) no BatchNorm (compensated by increased channel capacity $20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$); (3) minimal ONNX operators via static shapes. The architecture comprises 4 convolutional layers with gradual channel growth, global average pooling, and a 2-layer FC head (detailed specifications in Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}).

\textbf{Training.} Experts are trained independently with Adam optimizer and label smoothing. Non-Robust Training (NRT) uses standard cross-entropy; Robust Training (RT) employs PGD adversarial training ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 steps). See Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams} for full hyperparameters.

\textbf{Metrics.} Clean Accuracy (CA), Adversarial Accuracy (AA), Robustness Gap (ERP $=$ CA $-$ AA).

\textbf{ONNX Export.} BatchNorm folded into conv weights during export (see Appendix~\ref{app:onnx}), eliminating BatchNorm operators from the verification graph.

\subsection{Router (GatingNet)}

The router $\mathcal{G}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ performs dataset-level expert selection by outputting raw logits $g_i$ for each expert, with predicted meta-class $m^* = \arg\max_i g_i$. Architecture mirrors expert CNN (96.5K params) but outputs $N$ logits instead of class predictions.

\textbf{Unified Normalization.} To prevent distribution mismatch, the router uses dataset-averaged normalization: $\mu_{\text{unified}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \mu_i$, $\sigma_{\text{unified}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \sigma_i$.

\textbf{Training.} The router is trained after freezing experts using meta-class labels. NRT minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NRT}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,m)}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x), m)]$; RT adds adversarial loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RT}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{NRT}} + \mathbb{E}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x^{\text{adv}}), m)]$ with PGD-generated $x^{\text{adv}}$ ($\varepsilon = 8/255$).

\textbf{Verification Property.} For input $x$ with true meta-class $m^*$, verify $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$ for all $\|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon$ via VNNLIB counterexample search.

\textbf{Compositional Guarantee.} Verified router $+$ verified expert $\implies$ verified MoE, enabling modular certification.

\subsection{Training Paradigms and MoE Composition}

\textbf{Non-Robust Training (NRT).} Standard supervised learning with cross-entropy loss, achieving high clean accuracy but low adversarial~robustness.

\textbf{Robust Training (RT).} PGD adversarial training~\cite{madry2018,zhang2019trades} ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 iterations) trades clean accuracy for robustness.

\textbf{MetaMoE Loss.} Combined classification and gating objectives: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{gate}}$ where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ enforces correct class prediction and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{gate}}$ ensures correct expert selection.

\section{Compositional Verification with $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN}

Standard $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN verifies monolithic networks. Our MetaMoE system (router + 2~experts $\approx$~2M parameters) is intractable to verify as a whole due to scale and dynamic routing logic. We adopt a \textbf{compositional verification strategy} that decomposes the~problem:

\textbf{Key insight.} Router correctness is independent of expert computations. We verify the router maintains invariant expert selection within $\varepsilon$-balls, then verify experts independently. By Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Verified}_{\text{Router}}(x \to i) \land \text{Verified}_{\text{Expert}_i}(x \to j) \implies \text{Verified}_{\text{MoE}}(x \to j)
\end{equation}

This reduces verification from 2M to 96K parameters (20$\times$ reduction).

\subsection{Critical Design Decisions for Verifiability}

Five design decisions ensure router verifiability: (1)~\textbf{Router-only extraction} for 20$\times$ parameter reduction, (2)~\textbf{property negation} for counterexample search, (3)~\textbf{unified normalization} across datasets, (4)~\textbf{normalized-space perturbations} for threat model consistency, (5)~\textbf{BatchNorm elimination} for deterministic behavior. These preserve verification semantics while enabling $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~analysis.

\textbf{Metric Alignment: AA vs CRA for the Router.}
As will be quantified in Section~\ref{sec:gap-analysis}, router CRA ($\approx 100\%$) substantially exceeds end-to-end AA ($\approx 35\%$). This apparent discrepancy requires careful clarification of what each metric measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Router CRA} verifies the property: $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$ (correct expert selection) for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, on individual test samples drawn from CIFAR-10 and MNIST. Each sample is verified independently.
    \item \textbf{Router AA ($\approx 35\%$)} measures meta-class classification accuracy under PGD attack on the \emph{mixed} test set (CIFAR-10 $\cup$ MNIST), evaluated as end-to-end system accuracy (including expert predictions), not router-only argmax accuracy.
    \item \textbf{``Unknown''} in $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN indicates the verifier could not determine the property within the timeout (300s); it does not indicate a property violation.
\end{itemize}
The CRA $\gg$ AA pattern arises because CRA measures router-only invariance on well-separated samples, while AA measures end-to-end classification where expert errors dominate. The router achieves 100\% \emph{gating} accuracy under PGD (the expert selection is always correct), but the selected expert may misclassify, reducing end-to-end AA. This confirms that CRA and AA measure fundamentally different properties. See Appendix~\ref{app:gap} for detailed analysis.

    \item \textbf{Robust Training Enables Verification:} CIFAR-10-NRT is unverifiable; CIFAR-10-RT achieves 90\% verification at tight bounds. Adversarial training smooths decision boundaries, reducing bound pessimism.

    \item \textbf{MNIST-RT Achieves Near-Complete Verification:} 100\% at $\varepsilon = 2/255$, 99\% at $\varepsilon = 4/255$, and 95\% at $\varepsilon = 8/255$, validating that verification-aware architectures combined with adversarial training can achieve near-complete formal certification.

    \item \textbf{No Property Violations:} All verification failures are timeouts, not property violations, confirming that experts maintain decision correctness within perturbation bounds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Compositional Robustness via Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}}

The invariance condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} is satisfied empirically: routers maintain 100\% gating accuracy across all 40 runs. This means system robustness is determined entirely by expert quality: the 13.15\% adversarial accuracy gap between Both-RT and Both-NRT configurations ($\Delta_{\text{router}} < 0.1\%$) confirms the router acts as a transparent switch. The weakest expert constrains system robustness, enabling predictable component-level verification without full-system retraining.

The converse of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} does not hold: an MoE could be robust even with router switching, if all candidate experts agree. Our framework provides a conservative but practically useful sufficient condition.

\subsection{Empirical--Formal Association}

Within each domain, higher AA monotonically associates with higher CRA: MNIST NRT$\to$RT increases both; CIFAR-10 NRT is unverifiable while RT achieves 90\% CRA. However, the absolute gap $\Delta$ varies dramatically ($9\%$ for MNIST-RT vs.\ $73\%$ for CIFAR-10-RT), reflecting domain-dependent bound tightness rather than a universal correlation. For routers, the gap ($\sim$65\%) arises from a metric mismatch: CRA measures router-only invariance while AA captures end-to-end classification (Section~7.1).

\subsection{Limitations and Scope}

Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} applies to hard routing ($k=1$) only; soft/top-$k$ routing requires verifying invariance of the full expert set and weight stability. Current experiments use small verification-compatible CNNs (96.5K params); scaling to transformers faces memory constraints. Perfect gating relies on dataset dissimilarity; GTSRB+PTSD achieves only 93.17\% gating accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:similarity}), breaking the invariance assumption. Future work includes extensions to soft routing, probabilistic verification for larger architectures, and adaptive gating for similar domains.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a framework for compositional robustness verification of heterogeneous MoE systems. The central contribution is Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}: for hard-routed MoE, router invariance plus expert robustness is sufficient for system robustness. This decomposition reduces verification from 2M to 96K parameters and is validated both formally and empirically.

\textbf{Key findings:}
Firstly, for dissimilar datasets, expert robustness dominates system behavior (13.15\% adversarial accuracy difference), while router training shows negligible impact ($\Delta < 0.1\%$), skipping this enables 4.1$\times$ training speedup.
Secondly, router formal verification achieves 100\% success at practical threat models ($\varepsilon \leq 4/255$), satisfying the invariance condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} across all 40 experimental runs.
And thirdly, within each domain, higher empirical adversarial accuracy monotonically associates with higher certified robustness, with robust training as a prerequisite for verifiability.

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} The sufficient condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} does not extend to soft or top-$k > 1$ routing. Perfect gating relies on dataset dissimilarity; similar domains require stronger router guarantees. Future work includes probabilistic verification for larger architectures and extensions to soft routing composition.

\subsection{BatchNorm Folding}

For a convolutional layer with weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times k \times k}$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$, followed by BatchNorm with running mean $\mu$, running variance $\sigma^2$, learnable scale $\gamma$, shift $\beta$, and $\varepsilon_{\text{bn}} = 10^{-5}$, the folded parameters are:
\begin{align}
W'_{i,j,m,n} &= \gamma_i \cdot \frac{W_{i,j,m,n}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_i + \varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}} \quad \forall i,j,m,n \\
b'_i &= \frac{\gamma_i}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_i + \varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}} \cdot (b_i - \mu_i) + \beta_i \quad \forall i
\end{align}

Verification: $\max|f_{\text{original}}(x) - f_{\text{folded}}(x)| < 10^{-5}$ for random inputs. All models exported with PyTorch 2.0+, ONNX opset 13, static input shape $(1, 3, 32, 32)$, raw logits output.

\section{Detailed Architecture Specifications}
\label{app:architecture}

The VerifiableCNN architecture (96.5K parameters) used for both experts and router:

\textbf{Convolutional Feature Extraction:}
Conv Block 1: Conv2d($3 \to 20$, $3 \times 3$), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 20 \times 16 \times 16$.
Conv Block 2: Conv2d($20 \to 28$, $3 \times 3$), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 28 \times 8 \times 8$.
Conv Block 3: Conv2d($28 \to 40$, $3 \times 3$), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 40 \times 4 \times 4$.
Conv Block 4: Conv2d($40 \to 56$, $3 \times 3$), ReLU, Global AvgPool $\to 56$.

\textbf{Classification Head:} Linear($56 \to 128$), ReLU, Linear($128 \to C$) where $C = C_i$ for experts or $C = N$ for router. Output: raw logits (no softmax).

\textbf{Design Rationale:} Gradual channel growth ($3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$) compensates for absence of BatchNorm. Average pooling (linear operation) facilitates LP-based verification. No BatchNorm eliminates train/eval discrepancies. All $3 \times 3$ convolutions enable uniform bound propagation.

\section{Empirical--Formal Robustness Gap Analysis}
\label{app:gap}

CRA from formal verification often exceeds AA from PGD attacks. CRA measures the fraction of samples \textit{provably robust} within the $\varepsilon$-ball (all possible perturbations). AA measures robustness against \textit{specific attack sequences} (PGD with 7 iterations). PGD is a heuristic that may not find the worst-case adversarial example.

The gap $\Delta = \text{CRA} - \text{AA}$ varies: simple domains (MNIST-RT: $\Delta = 9.23\%$) achieve tight correspondence; complex domains (CIFAR-10-RT: $\Delta = 72.76\%$) exhibit loose correspondence; routers ($\Delta \approx 65\%$) reflect geometric dataset separation creating decision boundary margins $\gg \varepsilon$.

\textbf{Critical finding:} CIFAR-10-NRT exhibits CRA $= 0\%$ (complete verification failure), demonstrating robust training is a prerequisite for verifiability. Non-robust models produce excessively loose CROWN bounds due to chaotic decision boundaries.

\section{Dataset Similarity Analysis: GTSRB+PTSD vs.\ CIFAR10+MNIST}
\label{app:similarity}

GTSRB+PTSD (both traffic sign datasets) achieves only 93.17\% gating accuracy vs.\ 100\% for CIFAR10+MNIST. The 6.83\% routing error exceeds the margin explained by expert imbalance, indicating inherent domain confusion from shared visual structures.

Adversarial robustness gap: GTSRB+PTSD achieves 29.47\% adversarial accuracy vs.\ 41.59\% for CIFAR10+MNIST (12.12\% gap). Adversarial attacks exploit routing uncertainty more effectively when the router must distinguish between visually similar domains.

The compositional verification property (Verified router $\land$ Verified expert $\implies$ Verified system) holds trivially for dissimilar datasets (100\% gating accuracy) but requires additional verification effort for similar domains, where probabilistic guarantees may be needed.
