\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures offer modularity and scalability, yet their robustness and certifiability, especially in heterogeneous settings, remain poorly understood. This work presents \emph{MetaMoE}, a heterogeneous MoE framework designed for both adversarial robustness and formal verifiability under $\ell_\infty$-bounded perturbations. In MetaMoE, a learned routing network directs each input to a domain-specific expert; we study how robustness propagates compositionally across these components and establish when system-level verification can be derived from component-level verification. We prove that when the router maintains invariant expert selection within a perturbation region, expert-level robustness composes to full-system robustness, enabling scalable modular verification where per-component cost remains constant as the system grows (the reduction ratio scales linearly with the number of experts). We further show that expert robustness dominates system behavior for visually dissimilar domains, that empirical adversarial accuracy correlates with certified robustness, and that robust training is a prerequisite for verifiability. Experiments on heterogeneous datasets demonstrate that robust experts improve adversarial accuracy by up to 13.1 percentage points and enable higher formal certifiability, while verified routers achieve 100\% certified routing accuracy at practical perturbation budgets for dissimilar datasets. Together, these results show that empirical robustness testing and formal certification reinforce each other in heterogeneous MoE systems, and outline a principled path toward scalable, verifiable modular AI.

\keywords{Mixture-of-experts \and Adversarial Robustness \and Formal Verification \and Compositional Verification \and Neural Network Verification \and Safety-Critical AI}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) achieve high accuracy on image classification tasks but remain vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations~\cite{goodfellow2015,madry2018}. This unreliability is hard to justify in safety-critical vision applications such as autonomous driving, traffic-sign recognition, and medical imaging. In parallel, Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures~\cite{jacobs1991,shazeer2017}, originally proposed in the early 1990s and recently revitalized for large-scale deep learning, offer a powerful paradigm for scaling models through modular computation and conditional activation. While recent work has popularized MoE primarily within large language models~\cite{shazeer2017,deepseek2024}, the paradigm is equally natural for \emph{vision} systems~\cite{riquelme2021} where distinct visual domains (e.g., traffic signs, handwritten digits, natural images) benefit from dedicated expert networks. Yet the robustness properties of such heterogeneous vision MoE systems, where domain-specific experts must cooperate under a shared router, remain poorly understood.

In a heterogeneous vision MoE, a lightweight convolutional \emph{router} inspects each input image and dispatches it to one of several domain-specific \emph{experts}, each a CNN independently trained on a distinct image-classification task (e.g., traffic-sign recognition or digit classification). Formal verification tools such as $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~\cite{wang2021} can certify provable robustness for individual networks, yet verifying a full MoE system remains challenging due to its size, sparsity, and dynamic routing behavior. In this work, we ask whether certified guarantees \emph{compose}: can the verified robustness of individual experts and the router be combined to certify the full system? An affirmative answer would allow heterogeneous MoE systems to be trained, extended with new experts, and certified modularly, avoiding the need to re-verify the entire system whenever a single component changes.

This paper introduces \textsf{MetaMoE}, a verification-aware heterogeneous MoE architecture in which each input carries two labels: a fine-grained \emph{class} (e.g., speed-limit sign) and a coarse \emph{meta-class} identifying the domain (e.g., German traffic signs vs.\ handwritten digits). The router learns to predict the meta-class and dispatches the input to the corresponding domain expert, which then predicts the fine-grained class. This two-level classification motivates the name \emph{Meta}MoE. The framework combines verifiable experts with a verifiable routing network, intermixing adversarially and non-adversarially trained components. We investigate how empirical adversarial robustness, measured by accuracy under Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks~\cite{madry2018}, where higher accuracy indicates greater resilience to worst-case perturbations, relates to certified robustness (formal bounds), and establish conditions under which these guarantees propagate from individual components to the entire system. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Formalize a \emph{compositional robustness proposition}: for hard-routed MoE, where only one expert is activated at any given time, if the router maintains invariant expert selection within an $\ell_\infty$ ball and the selected expert is robust, then the full MoE is robust. We prove this sufficient condition and validate it~empirically.
    \item Identify an association between empirical adversarial accuracy and certified robustness within domain/architecture regimes, and demonstrating that robust training is a prerequisite for verifiability of large and complex neural network architectures.
    \item For visually dissimilar domains, we demonstrate that expert robustness dominates system behavior, while router adversarial training has negligible impact.
    \item We show that compositional verification significantly reduces verification temporal and spatial cost, enabling scalable modular certification and efficient incremental expert integration.
\end{itemize}

Together, these findings establish a unified empirical and formal understanding of robustness in heterogeneous MoE systems and provide a foundation for scalable, verifiable modular AI.

\section{Related Work}

Mixture-of-experts architectures route inputs through a gating network to specialized sub-models, enabling modular, scalable computation~\cite{jacobs1991}. Sparse gating~\cite{shazeer2017} made MoE practical at scale, and subsequent work extended the paradigm to vision~\cite{riquelme2021}, demonstrated that freezing pretrained experts reduces retraining cost for lifelong learning~\cite{fedus2022}, and scaled sparse routing to hundreds of billions of parameters~\cite{dai2024,jiang2024,deepseek2024}. Complementary advances in mitigating catastrophic forgetting~\cite{ramasesh2022}, evolving gating from unsupervised~\cite{jordan1994} to supervised~\cite{riquelme2021} schemes, and modular deep learning~\cite{pfeiffer2023} have collectively established MoE as a mature paradigm for composable model design.

Yet this scalability work largely ignores a critical question: \emph{are MoE systems robust, and can that robustness be formally certified?} The few studies that examine MoE robustness reach conflicting conclusions. Puigcerver et al.~\cite{puigcerver2022} reported that sparse MoE models can be \emph{more} robust than dense counterparts, while Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025moe} found expert networks \emph{more susceptible} to adversarial attacks. Pavlitska et al.~\cite{pavlitska2025robust} studied adversarial training on CNNs with sparse MoE layers, but focused on empirical accuracy rather than formal guarantees. Crucially, none of these works consider how robustness propagates through the router-expert composition, nor do they connect empirical resilience to provable certificates.

Meanwhile, neural network verification has matured as an independent line of research. Katz et al.~\cite{katz2017} established the NP-completeness of the general problem, motivating scalable approximations: $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~\cite{wang2021} provides state-of-the-art complete verification via bound propagation (winning VNN-COMP 2021--2025~\cite{kaulen2025}), NNV~\cite{tran2020nnv} offers reachability-based verification for learning-enabled cyber-physical systems, and certified training methods such as CROWN-IBP~\cite{zhang2020} and randomized smoothing~\cite{cohen2019} produce models with built-in provable guarantees. PGD-based adversarial training~\cite{madry2018} provides complementary empirical robustness. However, all of these tools and techniques target \emph{monolithic} networks; they cannot directly certify a system whose behavior depends on dynamic routing among multiple sub-networks.

A natural solution is compositional verification, long established in classical systems through assume-guarantee reasoning~\cite{henzinger1998}. Zhou and Tripakis~\cite{zhou2024compositional} recently adapted compositional inductive invariants to neural network controlled systems, demonstrating that modular proofs can scale where monolithic analysis cannot. However, their framework addresses feedback control loops, not the conditional-activation structure of MoE, where a router's decision determines which expert's guarantees apply.

These three lines of work -- MoE scalability, adversarial robustness, and formal verification -- have developed largely in isolation. MoE research scales models without certifying them; robustness studies attack or defend MoE empirically without formal guarantees; and verification certifies monolithic networks without addressing compositional, dynamically-routed architectures. This paper bridges all three by asking: \textit{Can compositional robustness in heterogeneous MoE systems be formally verified and empirically validated?} We answer affirmatively by proving that router invariance and expert robustness compose to yield system-level guarantees, and by showing that empirical adversarial accuracy is a reliable predictor of formal certifiability.

\section{Problem Formulation}

We define the following notation used throughout this paper. Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ denote a normalized input image, where $C = 3$ is the number of color channels (RGB), $H$ is the image height, and $W$ is the image width (in our experiments, $H = W = 32$ pixels). Let $N$ denote the total number of domain-specific experts in the system, and let $C_i$ denote the number of classes in dataset~$i$ (e.g., $C_i = 10$ for CIFAR-10, $C_i = 43$ for GTSRB). The total number of classes across all datasets is $C_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i$, and the target class label is $y \in \{1, \ldots, C_{\text{total}}\}$.

A heterogeneous MoE system consists of two types of components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experts:} Each expert $E_i$ is a neural network specialized for dataset~$i$. It maps an input image to a vector of class logits (unnormalized scores) for its domain: $E_i: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$, producing $y_i = E_i(x)$ where $y_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ is the logit vector for domain~$i$.
    \item \textbf{Router:} The router (also called gating network) $\mathcal{G}$ determines which expert should handle a given input. It maps the input image to $N$ routing logits: $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^N$, producing $g = \mathcal{G}(x)$ where $g_i$ is the routing score for expert~$i$.
\end{enumerate}

Under hard routing (top-1 selection), the router selects a single expert $m^* = \arg\max_i g_i$, i.e., the expert with the highest routing score. For top\nobreakdash-$k$ routing (where $k$ experts are activated simultaneously), let $\mathcal{I}_k(g) = \{i_1, \ldots, i_k\}$ denote the indices of the $k$ experts with the highest routing scores. Each selected expert~$i_j$ receives a normalized weight $w_j = g_{i_j} / \sum_{\ell=1}^k g_{i_\ell}$, representing its contribution to the final output. The MoE produces a unified output vector $\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{total}}}$ by placing each expert's weighted logits at the appropriate position using class offsets $o_i = \sum_{m<i} C_m$ (i.e., $o_i$ is the starting index for expert~$i$'s classes in the unified output):
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_c = \begin{cases}
        w_j \cdot y_{i_j}[c - o_{i_j}], & \text{if } c \in \{o_{i_j}, \ldots, o_{i_j} + C_{i_j} - 1\} \text{ for some selected expert } i_j \\
        0, & \text{otherwise (classes belonging to non-selected experts)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here, $\hat{y}_c$ is the $c$-th element of the unified output, $y_{i_j}[c - o_{i_j}]$ retrieves the logit from expert~$i_j$ at the local class index $c - o_{i_j}$, and $w_j$ is the routing weight for that expert.

\subsection{Adversarial Threat Model and Robustness}

We consider an adversary that applies a small perturbation $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ to the input, bounded under the $\ell_\infty$ norm: $\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon > 0$ is the perturbation budget (e.g., $\varepsilon = 8/255 \approx 0.031$ in pixel intensity). This produces adversarial examples $x' = x + \delta$ lying within the $\varepsilon$-ball $B_\varepsilon(x) = \{x' : \|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\}$, i.e., the set of all images within $\varepsilon$ of the original input in every pixel channel. Robustness requires that the model's prediction remains correct for \emph{all} perturbations within this ball:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expert $E_i$ robustness:} The expert's predicted class is unchanged: $\arg\max_c\, y_i(x')[c] = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, where $y_i(x') = E_i(x')$ is the logit vector of expert~$i$ on the perturbed input.
    \item \textbf{Router $\mathcal{G}$ robustness (invariance):} The router selects the same expert under perturbation: $m^*(x') = m^*(x)$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, or equivalently, the selected expert's routing logit remains dominant: $g_{m^*}(x') - g_j(x') > 0$ for all $j \neq m^*$ and all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$.
    \item \textbf{MoE system robustness:} The final classification is preserved: $\arg\max_c \hat{y}_c(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, where $\hat{y}(x')$ is the unified MoE output (Eq.~1).
\end{itemize}

We quantify verifiability using \emph{certified robustness accuracy} (CRA), which measures the fraction of test samples for which a formal verifier can prove robustness within the $\varepsilon$-ball:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D} \mathbbm{1}[\text{Verify}(f, x, \varepsilon)]
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the test dataset, $f$ denotes the model being verified (expert, router, or full MoE), and $\mathbbm{1}[\text{Verify}(f, x, \varepsilon)]$ is an indicator function that equals~1 if the verifier proves $f$ is robust at input~$x$ within radius~$\varepsilon$, and~0 otherwise (including timeouts).

\subsection{Compositional Robustness}

We state the central compositional property for hard routing ($k=1$, argmax selection) for experts with different classes:

\begin{definition}[Router Invariance]
Router $\mathcal{G}$ is \emph{invariant} at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ if $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Compositional Robustness -- Sufficient Condition]
\label{prop:compositional}
Consider a hard-routed MoE ($k{=}1$) with router $\mathcal{G}$ and experts $\{E_i\}$. Let $m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$ denote the selected expert for input~$x$. If:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] $\mathcal{G}$ is invariant at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ (router selects $m^*$ throughout), and
    \item[(ii)] $E_{m^*}$ is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ (i.e., $\arg\max_c E_{m^*,c}(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$),
\end{enumerate}
then the MoE system is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under hard routing, $\hat{y}_c(x') = E_{m^*(x'),c'}(x')$ for the appropriate class offset. By assumption~(i), $m^*(x') = m^*$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, so the same expert is evaluated. By assumption~(ii), the selected expert preserves the correct classification. Hence $\arg\max_c \hat{y}_c(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. \qed
\end{proof}

\textbf{Converse:} The converse does not hold in general: if multiple candidate experts share overlapping classes and agree on the same output, the MoE may produce the correct output even if the router switches between experts within $B_\varepsilon(x)$. However, when each expert covers a \emph{disjoint} set of classes, as in our setting, the converse does hold. Under hard routing, only the selected expert produces output; if the router switches to expert $m' \neq m^*$, the output lies in $m'$'s class range, which does not contain the true label $y$, so the MoE necessarily mis-routed or mis-classified. Therefore, with disjoint expert classes, Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} provides a necessary and sufficient condition: the MoE is robust at $x$ within $B_\varepsilon(x)$ if and only if both router invariance and expert robustness hold. For soft or top-$k$ routing ($k > 1$), the sufficient condition requires invariance of the full top-$k$ set and robustness of all selected experts, which we do not address here. Our experiments use $k=1$.

MoE's robustness, in our setting, decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MoE robust at } x \Longleftrightarrow \text{Router robust} \land \text{Expert } E_{m^*} \text{ robust}
\end{equation}

We also examine the case where experts handle visually similar datasets: specifically, GTSRB and PTSD (both traffic sign recognition tasks with 43 classes each), where the disjoint-class assumption still holds but the router faces a harder discrimination problem. We report these results in Appendix~\ref{app:similarity}.

This decomposition enables modular verification: verify router invariance and expert robustness independently, then compose guarantees via Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}.

\subsection{Empirical--Formal Gap}

We define the \emph{certification gap} $\Delta = \text{CRA}(\varepsilon) - \text{AA}(\varepsilon)$, which quantifies the divergence between formal verification and empirical adversarial evaluation. Here, $\text{AA}(\varepsilon)$ denotes the \emph{adversarial accuracy} -- the fraction of test samples correctly classified under a PGD attack with perturbation budget~$\varepsilon$ -- and $\text{CRA}(\varepsilon)$ is the certified robustness accuracy defined in Eq.~2. A positive gap ($\Delta > 0$) indicates that formal verification certifies more samples than empirical attacks can defeat, reflecting conservatism of the attack rather than looseness of the verifier. Note that CRA and AA may measure different properties (e.g., router invariance vs.\ end-to-end classification); see the Metric Alignment discussion in Section~7.1. We hypothesize that within a given domain and architecture, higher AA monotonically associates with higher CRA, with robust training as a prerequisite for and predictive of verifiability.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given $N$ pretrained experts $\{E_i\}$, router $\mathcal{G}$, and $L_\infty$ adversary with radius $\varepsilon$, we investigate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compositional Robustness:} Does robust training of components yield provably robust MoE composition?
    \item \textbf{Empirical--Formal Correlation:} Does PGD adversarial accuracy predict certified robustness accuracy?
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} Can modular robustness achieve reduced retraining time, linear inference $O(k)$, and stable verification?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hypotheses}

The following hypotheses are posited regarding the compositional robustness and scalability of a multi-dataset heterogeneous MoE architecture.

\noindent\textbf{H1 (Adversarial Robustness--Accuracy Trade-off).}
Adversarial training (AT) of both experts and the router exhibits higher robustness (smaller drop in adversarial accuracy under $L_\infty$ perturbation of radius $\varepsilon$) but lower clean accuracy on unperturbed inputs, compared to non-robustly trained (NRT) counterparts. This trade-off arises from the regularization effect of AT, which prioritizes decision boundary smoothness over fitting to clean data.

\noindent\textbf{H2 (Empirical Verification Translates to Formal Verification).}
Within a given domain and architecture, models with higher empirical adversarial accuracy (via PGD attacks) will achieve higher certified robustness accuracy when formally verified using $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN. Furthermore, robust training is a prerequisite: non-robustly trained models on complex domains will be significantly more expensive to verify, temporally and spatially. This creates a bridge between empirical-based verification and theoretical-based verification.

\noindent\textbf{H3 (Compositional Robustness).}
An MoE composed of experts and a router trained with robust methods exhibits higher overall robustness and lower clean accuracy compared to an MoE composed of non-robust components. This compositional property holds under the assumption that the router correctly selects experts even under perturbations.

\noindent\textbf{H4 (Training Time Scalability).}
Freezing pretrained experts and training only the router enables efficient incremental learning. As the number of experts grows, this compositional strategy reduces retraining cost relative to monolithic models.

\noindent\textbf{H5 (Linear Inference Scaling).}
The MoE inference cost scales linearly with the number of activated experts $k < N$, i.e., $O(k)$, since only routed experts are evaluated.

These hypotheses collectively address the core challenges of robustness and efficiency in heterogeneous MoE systems, guiding experiment setup decisions.

\section{Methodology}

Given an RGB input image $x \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32}$ (3 color channels, $32 \times 32$ pixels) and $N$ domain-specific datasets each containing $C_i$ classes, we independently train $N$ expert networks $E_i: \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ (one per dataset, frozen after training) and a single router $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 32 \times 32} \to \mathbb{R}^N$ that outputs one routing logit per expert. We evaluate on 7 datasets: CIFAR-10, MNIST, GTSRB (diverse domains~\cite{gtsrb}) and PTSD, BTSD, ETSD, TSRD (similar traffic sign domains for lifelong learning).

\subsection{Verification-Aware CNN Architecture}

Both experts and router use VerifiableCNN designed for $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN compatibility: (1) average pooling ($2{\times}2$) instead of max pooling for linearity; (2) increased channel capacity ($20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$) to compensate for narrow widths; (3) minimal ONNX operators via static shapes. Experts include BatchNorm (${\sim}$96K params for 43 classes); the router omits BatchNorm for deterministic verification behavior (${\sim}$38K params). The architecture comprises 4 convolutional layers with gradual channel growth, $2{\times}2$ average pooling in blocks 1--3, and a 2-layer FC head (detailed specifications in Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}).

\textbf{Training.} Experts are trained independently with Adam optimizer and label smoothing. Non-Robust Training (NRT) uses standard cross-entropy; Robust Training (RT) employs PGD adversarial training ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 steps). See Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams} for full hyperparameters.

\textbf{Metrics.} Clean Accuracy (CA), Adversarial Accuracy (AA), Robustness Gap (ERP $=$ CA $-$ AA).

\textbf{ONNX Export.} BatchNorm folded into conv weights during export (see Appendix~\ref{app:onnx}), eliminating BatchNorm operators from the verification graph.

\subsection{Router (GatingNet)}

The router $\mathcal{G}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ performs dataset-level expert selection by outputting $N$ raw logits (one routing score $g_i$ per expert), with predicted meta-class $m^* = \arg\max_i g_i$ (the expert receiving the highest score). The architecture shares the same convolutional backbone as the expert CNN but omits BatchNorm (for deterministic verification behavior), giving ${\sim}$38K parameters for $N{=}2$. Its final layer outputs $N$ logits (number of experts) instead of $C_i$ class logits.

\textbf{Unified Normalization.} Since the router receives images from multiple datasets with different pixel distributions, we normalize all inputs using dataset-averaged statistics: $\mu_{\text{unified}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \mu_i$ and $\sigma_{\text{unified}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \sigma_i$, where $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ are the per-channel mean and standard deviation of dataset~$i$. This prevents distribution mismatch at the router input.

\textbf{Training.} The router is trained after freezing all experts, using meta-class labels $m \in \{0, \ldots, N{-}1\}$ that identify which dataset each sample belongs to. NRT minimizes the cross-entropy between router predictions and true meta-class: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NRT}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,m)}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x), m)]$; RT additionally includes the loss on adversarial examples: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RT}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{NRT}} + \mathbb{E}[\text{CE}(\mathcal{G}(x^{\text{adv}}), m)]$, where $x^{\text{adv}}$ is generated by PGD attack ($\varepsilon = 8/255$).

\textbf{Verification Property.} For input $x$ with true meta-class $m^*$, verify $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$ for all $\|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon$ via VNNLIB counterexample search.

\textbf{Compositional Guarantee.} Verified router $+$ verified expert $\implies$ verified MoE, enabling modular certification.

\subsection{Training Paradigms and MoE Composition}

\textbf{Non-Robust Training (NRT).} Standard supervised learning with cross-entropy loss, achieving high clean accuracy but low adversarial~robustness.

\textbf{Robust Training (RT).} PGD adversarial training~\cite{madry2018,zhang2019trades} ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 iterations) trades clean accuracy for robustness.

\textbf{MetaMoE Loss.} Combined classification and gating objectives: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{gate}}$ where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ enforces correct class prediction and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{gate}}$ ensures correct expert selection.

\section{Compositional Verification with $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN}

Standard $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN verifies monolithic networks. Our MetaMoE system (router + 2~experts $\approx$~231K parameters) benefits from a \textbf{compositional verification strategy} that decomposes the~problem:

\textbf{Key insight.} Router correctness is independent of expert computations. We verify the router maintains invariant expert selection within $\varepsilon$-balls, then verify each expert independently. By Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}, if both components are verified, the full system is verified:
\begin{equation}
\text{Verified}_{\text{Router}}(x \to i) \land \text{Verified}_{\text{Expert}_i}(x \to j) \implies \text{Verified}_{\text{MoE}}(x \to j)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Verified}_{\text{Router}}(x \to i)$ means the router provably selects expert~$i$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, and $\text{Verified}_{\text{Expert}_i}(x \to j)$ means expert~$i$ provably predicts class~$j$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$.

This reduces verification from the full system (${\sim}$231K parameters for $N{=}2$ experts) to the largest single component (${\sim}$96K per expert or ${\sim}$38K for the router), since each component is verified independently. The per-component verification cost is \emph{constant} regardless of system size: monolithic cost grows as $O(N)$ while each verification task remains fixed at the size of a single component. In our $N{=}2$ setting this yields a ${\sim}$2.4$\times$ reduction, but the advantage scales linearly with $N$ -- for $N{=}5$ the reduction is ${\sim}$5$\times$, for $N{=}10$ roughly ${\sim}$10$\times$ -- making our experiments a conservative lower bound on the compositional advantage.

\subsection{Critical Design Decisions for Verifiability}

Five design decisions ensure router verifiability: (1)~\textbf{Router-only extraction} (${\sim}$38K params vs.\ ${\sim}$231K full system), (2)~\textbf{property negation} for counterexample search, (3)~\textbf{unified normalization} across datasets, (4)~\textbf{normalized-space perturbations} for threat model consistency, (5)~\textbf{BatchNorm elimination} for deterministic behavior. These preserve verification semantics while enabling $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN~analysis.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\subsection{Formal Verification with $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN}

\subsubsection{Overview.}

Formal verification of neural networks is critical for safety-critical applications like autonomous vehicle decision-making. We used $\alpha$\nobreakdash--$\beta$\nobreakdash-CROWN, the state-of-the-art neural network verifier and winner of VNN-COMP 2021--2025~\cite{wang2021,xu2020abcrown,kaulen2025}, to provide provable robustness guarantees for both individual experts and the router.

\textbf{Metric Alignment: AA vs CRA for the Router.}
As will be quantified in Section~\ref{sec:gap-analysis}, router CRA ($\approx 100\%$) substantially exceeds end-to-end AA ($\approx 35\%$). This apparent discrepancy requires careful clarification of what each metric measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Router CRA} verifies the property: $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$ (correct expert selection) for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, on individual test samples drawn from CIFAR-10 and MNIST. Each sample is verified independently.
    \item \textbf{Router AA ($\approx 35\%$)} measures meta-class classification accuracy under PGD attack on the \emph{mixed} test set (CIFAR-10 $\cup$ MNIST), evaluated as end-to-end system accuracy (including expert predictions), not router-only argmax accuracy.
    \item \textbf{``Unknown''} in $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN indicates the verifier could not determine the property within the timeout (300s); it does not indicate a property violation.
\end{itemize}
The CRA $\gg$ AA pattern arises because CRA measures router-only invariance on well-separated samples, while AA measures end-to-end classification where expert errors dominate. The router achieves 100\% \emph{gating} accuracy under PGD (the expert selection is always correct), but the selected expert may misclassify, reducing end-to-end AA. This confirms that CRA and AA measure fundamentally different properties. See Appendix~\ref{app:gap} for detailed analysis.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Compositional Robustness via Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}}

The invariance condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} is satisfied empirically: routers maintain 100\% gating accuracy across all 40 runs. This means system robustness is determined entirely by expert quality: the 13.15\% adversarial accuracy gap between Both-RT and Both-NRT configurations ($\Delta_{\text{router}} < 0.1\%$) confirms the router acts as a transparent switch. The weakest expert constrains system robustness, enabling predictable component-level verification without full-system retraining. Notably, our $N{=}2$ experiments yield a modest ${\sim}$2.4$\times$ verification reduction, but this advantage grows linearly with $N$: with 7 datasets (Table~\ref{tab:notation}) the reduction reaches ${\sim}$7$\times$, since monolithic cost scales with the total system while per-component cost remains fixed.

The converse of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} does not hold: an MoE could be robust even with router switching, if all candidate experts agree. Our framework provides a conservative but practically useful sufficient condition.

\subsection{Empirical--Formal Association}

Within each domain, higher AA monotonically associates with higher CRA: MNIST NRT$\to$RT increases both; CIFAR-10 NRT is unverifiable while RT achieves 90\% CRA. However, the absolute gap $\Delta$ varies dramatically ($9\%$ for MNIST-RT vs.\ $73\%$ for CIFAR-10-RT), reflecting domain-dependent bound tightness rather than a universal correlation. For routers, the gap ($\sim$65\%) arises from a metric mismatch: CRA measures router-only invariance while AA captures end-to-end classification (Section~7.1).

\subsection{Limitations and Scope}

Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} applies to hard routing ($k=1$) only; soft/top-$k$ routing requires verifying invariance of the full expert set and weight stability. Current experiments use small verification-compatible CNNs (${\sim}$96K params per expert, ${\sim}$38K for the router); scaling to transformers faces memory constraints. Perfect gating relies on dataset dissimilarity; GTSRB+PTSD achieves only 93.17\% gating accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:similarity}), breaking the invariance assumption. Future work includes extensions to soft routing, probabilistic verification for larger architectures, and adaptive gating for similar domains.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a framework for compositional robustness verification of heterogeneous MoE systems. The central contribution is Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional}: for hard-routed MoE, router invariance plus expert robustness is sufficient for system robustness. This decomposition reduces verification from the full system to the largest single component, with the reduction ratio scaling linearly with the number of experts (${\sim}$2.4$\times$ at $N{=}2$, ${\sim}$7$\times$ at $N{=}7$, ${\sim}N$ asymptotically), and is validated both formally and empirically.

\textbf{Key findings:}
Firstly, for dissimilar datasets, expert robustness dominates system behavior (13.15\% adversarial accuracy difference), while router training shows negligible impact ($\Delta < 0.1\%$), skipping this enables 3.2$\times$ training speedup.
Secondly, router formal verification achieves 100\% success at practical threat models ($\varepsilon \leq 4/255$), satisfying the invariance condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} across all 40 experimental runs.
And thirdly, within each domain, higher empirical adversarial accuracy monotonically associates with higher certified robustness, with robust training as a prerequisite for verifiability.

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} The sufficient condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:compositional} does not extend to soft or top-$k > 1$ routing. Perfect gating relies on dataset dissimilarity; similar domains require stronger router guarantees. Future work includes probabilistic verification for larger architectures and extensions to soft routing composition.

\section{ONNX Export Details}
\label{app:onnx}

\subsection{BatchNorm Folding}

For a convolutional layer with weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times k \times k}$ (where $C_{\text{out}}$ is the number of output channels, $C_{\text{in}}$ the number of input channels, and $k \times k$ the kernel size) and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$, followed by a BatchNorm layer with running mean $\mu_i$, running variance $\sigma^2_i$, learnable scale $\gamma_i$, and shift $\beta_i$ (all per output channel~$i$), with numerical stability constant $\varepsilon_{\text{bn}} = 10^{-5}$, the folded (merged) parameters are:
\begin{align}
W'_{i,j,m,n} &= \gamma_i \cdot \frac{W_{i,j,m,n}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_i + \varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}} \quad \forall i,j,m,n \\
b'_i &= \frac{\gamma_i}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_i + \varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}} \cdot (b_i - \mu_i) + \beta_i \quad \forall i
\end{align}
Here, the index $i$ ranges over output channels ($1 \leq i \leq C_{\text{out}}$), $j$ over input channels, and $m, n$ over spatial kernel dimensions. The folded layer $\text{Conv}(W', b')$ is mathematically equivalent to $\text{BatchNorm}(\text{Conv}(W, b))$, eliminating the BatchNorm operator from the ONNX graph.

Verification: $\max|f_{\text{original}}(x) - f_{\text{folded}}(x)| < 10^{-5}$ for random inputs. All models exported with PyTorch 2.0+, ONNX opset 13, static input shape $(1, 3, 32, 32)$, raw logits output.

\section{Detailed Architecture Specifications}
\label{app:architecture}

The VerifiableCNN architecture used for experts (${\sim}$96K parameters with BatchNorm, 43 classes; ${\sim}$94K for 10 classes) and router (${\sim}$38K parameters without BatchNorm):

\textbf{Convolutional Feature Extraction:}
Conv Block 1: Conv2d($3 \to 20$, $3 \times 3$, padding=1), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 20 \times 16 \times 16$.
Conv Block 2: Conv2d($20 \to 28$, $3 \times 3$, padding=1), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 28 \times 8 \times 8$.
Conv Block 3: Conv2d($28 \to 40$, $3 \times 3$, padding=1), ReLU, AvgPool($2 \times 2$) $\to 40 \times 4 \times 4$.
Conv Block 4: Conv2d($40 \to 56$, $3 \times 3$, padding=1), ReLU $\to 56 \times 4 \times 4$.

\textbf{Classification Head:} Flatten($56 \times 4 \times 4 = 896$), Linear($896 \to 64$), ReLU, Dropout($0.3$), Linear($64 \to C$) where $C = C_i$ for experts or $C = N$ for router. Output: raw logits (no softmax).

\textbf{Expert vs.\ Router:} Experts include BatchNorm after each convolution (${\sim}$96K params for 43 classes); the router omits BatchNorm for deterministic verification behavior (${\sim}$38K params for $N{=}2$).

\textbf{Design Rationale:} Gradual channel growth ($3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$) compensates for narrow channels. Average pooling (linear operation) facilitates LP-based verification. Router omits BatchNorm to eliminate train/eval discrepancies. All $3 \times 3$ convolutions enable uniform bound propagation.

\section{Empirical--Formal Robustness Gap Analysis}
\label{app:gap}

CRA from formal verification often exceeds AA from PGD attacks. CRA measures the fraction of samples \textit{provably robust} within the $\varepsilon$-ball (all possible perturbations). AA measures robustness against \textit{specific attack sequences} (PGD with 7 iterations). PGD is a heuristic that may not find the worst-case adversarial example.

The gap $\Delta = \text{CRA} - \text{AA}$ varies: simple domains (MNIST-RT: $\Delta = 9.23\%$) achieve tight correspondence; complex domains (CIFAR-10-RT: $\Delta = 72.76\%$) exhibit loose correspondence; routers ($\Delta \approx 65\%$) reflect geometric dataset separation creating decision boundary margins $\gg \varepsilon$.

\textbf{Critical finding:} CIFAR-10-NRT exhibits CRA $= 0\%$ (complete verification failure), demonstrating robust training is a prerequisite for verifiability. Non-robust models produce excessively loose CROWN bounds due to chaotic decision boundaries.

\section{Dataset Similarity Analysis: GTSRB+PTSD vs.\ CIFAR10+MNIST}
\label{app:similarity}

GTSRB+PTSD (both traffic sign datasets) achieves only 93.17\% gating accuracy vs.\ 100\% for CIFAR10+MNIST. The 6.83\% routing error exceeds the margin explained by expert imbalance, indicating inherent domain confusion from shared visual structures.

Adversarial robustness gap: GTSRB+PTSD achieves 29.47\% adversarial accuracy vs.\ 41.59\% for CIFAR10+MNIST (12.12\% gap). Adversarial attacks exploit routing uncertainty more effectively when the router must distinguish between visually similar domains.

The compositional verification property (Verified router $\land$ Verified expert $\implies$ Verified system) holds trivially for dissimilar datasets (100\% gating accuracy) but requires additional verification effort for similar domains, where probabilistic guarantees may be needed.
