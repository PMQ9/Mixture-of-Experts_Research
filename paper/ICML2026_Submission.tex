% ABSTRACT
%==============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
\textit{Heterogeneous} Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where domain-specific experts with distinct training data or architectures are coordinated by a learned router, offer modularity and scalability, yet the interaction between routing stability and expert robustness remains underexplored. Verifying full MoE systems is intractable: dynamic routing induces combinatorial behavior, and model scale overwhelms current verifier capacity. We present a \textbf{compositional verification framework} that decomposes this problem: if the router provably maintains consistent expert selection within an adversarial perturbation region, and the selected expert is provably robust, then the entire MoE is provably robust---reducing verification from the full system to independent components (20$\times$ parameter reduction). Empirically, we show that expert robustness dominates system behavior (13.1 percentage point improvement with adversarial training) while router training has negligible impact ($<$0.1\%) for dissimilar domains. Verified routers achieve 100\% certified accuracy at practical threat models. These results establish a principled path toward scalable, formally verified modular AI.
\end{abstract}

% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

\textit{Heterogeneous} Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models combine domain-specific experts, each potentially with different architectures or training data, coordinated by a learned router. This modularity enables scalable capacity, but conditional execution yields input-dependent architectures with intertwined discrete and continuous behavior. Formal neural network verification, which provides sound guarantees over all inputs in a specified set, already faces scalability challenges for standard networks. MoE architectures further induce a combinatorial explosion from expert selection and routing boundaries. Existing verifiers either fail to scale or rely on coarse relaxations that produce vacuous guarantees. We address this challenge by exploiting the structured partitioning of the input space induced by MoE gating, enabling sound and scalable verification through explicit reasoning over routing regions and expert-level decoupling.

Formal verification of neural networks provides provable robustness guarantees for safety-critical deployment. However, verifying MoE systems poses unique challenges: dynamic routing creates input-dependent computation paths, and the scale of standard MoEs exceeds the capacity of current complete verifiers like $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN. Complete neural network verification is NP-complete \citep{katz2017}, with complexity scaling exponentially in network size. While state-of-the-art verifiers like $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN can handle networks up to a few million parameters on specialized benchmarks \citep{brix2024}, typical MoE systems with hundreds of millions to billions of parameters remain fundamentally intractable. To make verification tractable, we treat routing and expert prediction as separable subproblems and show that their guarantees compose cleanly.

We address this challenge through a \textbf{compositional verification} approach. Our central result is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Compositional Robustness Theorem:} \textit{For top-1 routing MoE, if the router maintains invariant expert selection within an $\ell_\infty$ perturbation ball, and the selected expert is robust within that ball, then the MoE system is robust.}
\end{quote}

This decomposition is the key insight: rather than verifying the intractable full system, we verify components independently and compose guarantees. The practical impact is a \textbf{20$\times$ reduction} in verification complexity (2M $\to$ 96K parameters in our experiments).

Unlike homogeneous MoEs where experts share structure and training distribution, heterogeneous systems require the router to perform \textit{domain identification}, a distinct task from within-domain classification. This separation is key to our compositional approach: domain identification typically produces large decision margins, enabling tractable router verification.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compositional Verification Framework for Heterogeneous MoE:} \textbf{20$\times$ verification speedup} is achieved by formalizing conditions under which heterogeneous MoE robustness decomposes into router stability and expert robustness, enabling incremental expert integration without re-verifying the full system.

    \item \textbf{Expert Dominance Finding:} \textbf{13.1 percentage point} adversarial accuracy improvement stems from expert training, which determines system robustness; router adversarial training has negligible impact ($<$0.1\%) for visually dissimilar domains, enabling \textbf{4$\times$ training speedup}.

    \item \textbf{Verification-Aware Design:} We identify architectural requirements for tractable heterogeneous MoE verification and demonstrate that adversarial training is a \textit{prerequisite} for formal verifiability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Mixture-of-Experts.} The MoE paradigm \citep{jacobs1991} decomposes problems into specialized subtasks via gating networks. \citet{shazeer2017} scaled this through sparse top-$k$ routing, \citet{fedus2022} introduced Switch Transformers for trillion-parameter models, and \citet{riquelme2021} extended MoE to vision. Recent work has dramatically advanced MoE architectures: DeepSeekMoE \citep{dai2024} introduced fine-grained expert segmentation with shared experts for ultimate specialization, while Mixtral \citep{jiang2024} demonstrated sparse routing with 8 experts at production scale. DeepSeek-V3 \citep{deepseek2024} scaled to 671B parameters with auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, and DeepSeek-V3.2 \citep{deepseek2025v32} further introduced DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) for efficient long-context processing. Gemini \citep{team2024gemini} deployed sparse MoE with trillions of parameters and native multimodal support. These advances motivate verification: as MoEs power safety-critical applications, formal robustness guarantees become essential.

\paragraph{Adversarial Robustness of MoE.} Recent work establishes that MoEs exhibit distinct robustness characteristics. \citet{puigcerver2022} proved MoEs are provably more robust than dense counterparts for linear experts and demonstrated empirical robustness gains on ImageNet. \citet{zhang2025moe} found that expert networks are more susceptible to adversarial attacks than routers, proposing dual-model strategies for robustness-accuracy trade-offs. \citet{pavlitska2025robust} showed that adversarial training with sparse MoE layers improves robustness through expert specialization. However, these works provide empirical analysis rather than formal verification guarantees.

\paragraph{Neural Network Verification.} Complete verifiers like $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN \citep{wang2021}, winner of VNN-COMP 2021--2025 \citep{brix2025}, provide sound robustness certificates via bound propagation and branch-and-bound. Certified training methods (CROWN-IBP \citep{zhang2020}, SABR \citep{mueller2023}) improve verifiability by training with formal bounds. Randomized smoothing \citep{cohen2019} offers probabilistic certificates for larger models. Recent advances include incremental certificate learning \citep{duong2025neuralsat} for scalable piecewise-linear verification. However, all approaches face scalability limits with multi-million parameter systems.

\paragraph{Compositional Verification.} Compositional approaches decompose verification into manageable subproblems. Classical control theory provides foundations through passivity, dissipativity, and small-gain theorems for interconnected systems \citep{willems1972,zames1966}, while bisimulation relations enable behavioral abstraction in formal methods \citep{milner1989}. Modular deep learning \citep{pfeiffer2023} provides a framework for composable neural architectures. In robotics and hybrid systems, assume-guarantee reasoning enables modular certificates \citep{henzinger1998}. For neural network controlled systems, \citet{zhou2023compositional} introduced inductive invariant decomposition. MoEs are uniquely suited to compositional verification because the router explicitly partitions inputs into disjoint expert domains---if this partitioning is stable under perturbation, component guarantees compose directly.

\paragraph{Research Gap.} Prior work addresses MoE scalability \citep{dai2024,deepseek2024}, MoE adversarial robustness \citep{puigcerver2022,zhang2025moe}, or monolithic neural network verification \citep{wang2021} separately. We provide the first compositional verification framework specifically for \textit{heterogeneous} MoE systems, where domain separation enables tractable router verification and expert-level decomposition bridges modular architecture design with formal robustness guarantees.

% 3. COMPOSITIONAL VERIFICATION FRAMEWORK
%==============================================================================
\section{Compositional Verification Framework}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

\paragraph{Definitions.} We distinguish three key properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Robustness:} A model is $\varepsilon$-robust at input $x$ if its prediction remains correct for all perturbations $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. We measure this via \textit{certified accuracy} (formally verified) and \textit{adversarial accuracy} (empirical, against PGD attacks).
    \item \textbf{Router Stability:} The router is stable at $x$ if expert selection remains unchanged for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, i.e., $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$.
    \item \textbf{Certified vs.\ Adversarial Accuracy:} Certified accuracy counts inputs with formal robustness proofs; adversarial accuracy counts inputs where heuristic attacks (e.g., PGD) fail. Certified accuracy $\leq$ adversarial accuracy, as certification is strictly stronger.
\end{itemize}
We also distinguish training regimes: \textbf{Robust Training (RT)} uses adversarial examples during training \citep{madry2018}; \textbf{Non-Robust Training (NRT)} uses standard supervised learning without adversarial augmentation.

\paragraph{Notation.} Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be an input with class label $y \in \{1, \ldots, C\}$ where $C = \sum_{i=1}^N C_i$ is the total number of classes across all expert domains. We define the \textbf{$\ell_\infty$ perturbation ball} $B_\varepsilon(x) = \{x': \|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\}$, where $\|z\|_\infty = \max_i |z_i|$ bounds the maximum per-pixel change. For images with pixel values in $[0,1]$, $\varepsilon = 8/255 \approx 0.031$ represents imperceptible perturbations.

A heterogeneous MoE consists of $N$ domain-specific experts $\{E_i: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}\}_{i=1}^N$ and a router $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^N$ selecting expert $m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$.

\subsection{Main Result}

We now formalize the compositional robustness guarantee.

\begin{theorem}[Compositional Robustness]
\label{thm:compositional}
The robustness of a heterogeneous MoE system decomposes into two independently verifiable conditions: router stability (expert selection invariance) and expert robustness (correct classification within the perturbation region).

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Formal Statement.} Let $\mathrm{MoE} = (\mathcal{G}, \{E_i\})$ use top-1 routing and let
$m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$.
If
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stability}
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^* \;\text{(stability)}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robustness}
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y \;\text{(robustness),}
\end{equation}
then the MoE is $\varepsilon$-robust at $x$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Intuition.}
The router partitions the input space into disjoint expert regions. If these partitions are \textit{stable}---meaning the router satisfies $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, so no adversarial perturbation can alter expert selection---and the selected expert maintains correct classification throughout $B_\varepsilon(x)$, then the composed system inherits robustness without requiring monolithic verification.

\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch]
Let $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$ be an arbitrary perturbation. By the stability condition~\eqref{eq:stability}, the router selects expert $m^*$ for all inputs in $B_\varepsilon(x)$, hence $\mathrm{MoE}(x') = E_{m^*}(x')$. By the robustness condition~\eqref{eq:robustness}, expert $E_{m^*}$ correctly classifies all such perturbations: $\arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y$. Since the MoE output is determined solely by the selected expert, we have $\arg\max \mathrm{MoE}(x') = y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, establishing $\varepsilon$-robustness. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Top-1 Requirement]
Theorem~\ref{thm:compositional} requires top-1 routing. For top-$k$ routing ($k > 1$), the weighted combination of multiple experts introduces additional attack surface: adversaries could exploit the weighting mechanism even if individual experts are robust. Extending compositional guarantees to top-$k$ requires verifying both expert selection stability \textit{and} weight stability---a stronger condition we leave to future work.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Verification Implementation}

We use $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN for component verification:

\textbf{Router Verification:} Router stability \eqref{eq:stability} requires that expert $m^*$ remains the argmax throughout $B_\varepsilon(x)$. This holds if and only if $\mathcal{G}_{m^*}(x') > \mathcal{G}_j(x')$ for all competing experts $j \neq m^*$ and all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. We verify this by checking $\mathcal{G}_{m^*}(x') - \mathcal{G}_j(x') > 0$, reducing stability to $N-1$ binary properties per input. Expert verification follows the standard formulation \eqref{eq:robustness}.

\textbf{Challenges:} Router verification difficulty depends on decision margin geometry. When domains are visually dissimilar (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs MNIST), routers learn large margins ($\gg \varepsilon$), enabling easy certification. Similar domains produce smaller margins and harder verification (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Verification-Aware Architecture}

Both router and experts use \textbf{VerifiableCNN} (96K parameters) designed for $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN: average pooling (linear, unlike max pooling which introduces additional branching), no BatchNorm (folded into weights), and minimal operators. Training uses PGD adversarial training ($\varepsilon = 8/255$) for robust variants. Full specifications in Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}.

\section{Experiments}

We evaluate on CIFAR-10 and MNIST because their feature manifolds are nearly disjoint, a setting where router margins are large---allowing us to isolate the contribution of expert robustness. Extended experiments on similar domains appear in Appendix~\ref{app:similar_domains}.

\subsection{Scalability}

\textbf{20$\times$ parameter reduction} (2M full MoE $\to$ 96K largest component) and \textbf{4.6s average verification time} per sample at practical $\varepsilon$ are achieved through compositional verification. New experts integrate by verifying only the new component---existing verifications remain valid. \textbf{16.7\% training efficiency improvement} is achieved for 3-expert systems via frozen-expert router fine-tuning.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{When Does Compositional Verification Apply?} The framework requires router stability within $B_\varepsilon(x)$. For dissimilar domains (CIFAR-10 vs MNIST), routers learn large decision margins enabling easy certification. For similar domains (e.g., GTSRB vs PTSD traffic signs), routing accuracy degrades to 93\%, and compositional guarantees degrade unless the router achieves sufficiently large margins; this may require stronger adversarial training or probabilistic certification.

\paragraph{Architecture Scope.} Our verification-compatible CNNs (96K parameters) demonstrate the framework but limit expressiveness. Scaling to Transformers faces verifier memory constraints. Promising directions include verification-aware architecture search and combining modular verification with randomized smoothing for probabilistic certificates on larger models.

\paragraph{Relationship to Empirical Robustness.} Adversarial training improves certifiability by smoothing decision boundaries, reducing bound looseness in CROWN analysis. This explains why robust training simultaneously improves empirical adversarial accuracy and formal certification rates.

% 6. CONCLUSION
%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We establish that heterogeneous MoE robustness composes modularly under top-1 routing: verified router stability combined with verified expert robustness yields verified system robustness.

Our experiments reveal \textbf{13.1 percentage point} accuracy improvement from expert robustness, which dominates system behavior, while router training is negligible for dissimilar domains ($<$0.1\%). Critically, robust training is a prerequisite for formal verifiability---non-robust components produce decision boundaries incompatible with sound verification.

These results demonstrate that compositional design enables tractable formal verification of modular AI systems. Future work includes extending guarantees to top-$k$ routing and scaling to transformer-based architectures.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:compositional}}
\label{app:proof}

\begin{proof}
We prove that under the stated conditions, the MoE system is $\varepsilon$-robust at input $x$ with label $y$.

\textbf{Setup.} Let $\mathrm{MoE} = (\mathcal{G}, \{E_i\}_{i=1}^N)$ be a heterogeneous mixture-of-experts with top-1 routing. For input $x$, let $m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$ denote the selected expert. The MoE prediction is defined as:
\[
\mathrm{MoE}(x) = E_{m^*}(x)
\]

\textbf{Goal.} Show that $\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max \mathrm{MoE}(x') = y$.

\textbf{Proof.} Let $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$ be an arbitrary perturbation satisfying $\|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon$.

\textit{Step 1 (Router Stability).} By assumption~\eqref{eq:stability}:
\[
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*
\]
Thus, the router selects expert $E_{m^*}$ for all perturbations in $B_\varepsilon(x)$. This implies:
\[
\mathrm{MoE}(x') = E_{m^*}(x') \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)
\]

\textit{Step 2 (Expert Robustness).} By assumption~\eqref{eq:robustness}:
\[
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y
\]
The selected expert correctly classifies all perturbations within the $\varepsilon$-ball.

\textit{Step 3 (Composition).} Combining Steps 1 and 2:
\[
\arg\max \mathrm{MoE}(x') = \arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y \quad \forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)
\]

Therefore, the MoE system maintains correct classification for all inputs in $B_\varepsilon(x)$, satisfying the definition of $\varepsilon$-robustness.
\end{proof}

\section{Architecture and Training}
\label{app:architecture}

\textbf{VerifiableCNN (96K parameters):} 4 convolutional blocks ($3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$ channels), $3 \times 3$ kernels, ReLU activations, average pooling. Global average pooling $\to$ FC(56, 128) $\to$ ReLU $\to$ FC(128, $C$). No BatchNorm layers (folded into convolutional weights for ONNX export).

\textbf{Training:} Adam optimizer, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 128. Experts: 200 epochs; Router: 50 epochs on frozen experts. RT variants: PGD adversarial training ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 steps, step size $2/255$).

\textbf{Hardware:} Intel i7-14700K CPU, NVIDIA RTX 4060 8GB GPU, 64GB RAM. PyTorch 2.1, $\alpha$,$\beta$-CROWN (December 2024 release), Gurobi 11.0 LP solver. Verification timeout: 300 seconds per sample.
