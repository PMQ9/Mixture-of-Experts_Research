% ABSTRACT 
%==============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures offer modularity and scalability, yet the interaction between routing stability and expert robustness in heterogeneous MoEs remains underexplored. Verifying full MoE systems is intractable: dynamic routing induces combinatorial behavior, and system scale exceeds current verifier capacity. We present a \textbf{compositional verification framework} that decomposes this problem: if the router provably maintains consistent expert selection within an adversarial perturbation region, and the selected expert is provably robust, then the entire MoE is provably robust---reducing verification from the full system to independent components (20$\times$ parameter reduction).

Empirically, we show that expert robustness dominates system behavior (13.1 percentage point improvement with adversarial training) while router training has negligible impact ($<$0.1\%) for dissimilar domains. Verified routers achieve 100\% certified accuracy at practical threat models. These results establish a principled path toward scalable, formally verified modular AI.
\end{abstract}

%==============================================================================
% 1. INTRODUCTION 
%==============================================================================
\section{Introduction}

Formal verification of neural networks provides provable robustness guarantees critical for safety-critical deployment. However, verifying Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) systems poses unique challenges: dynamic routing creates input-dependent computation paths, and the scale of typical MoEs exceeds the capacity of current complete verifiers like $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN.

We address this challenge through a \textbf{compositional verification} approach. Our central result is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Compositional Robustness Theorem:} \textit{For top-1 routing MoE, if the router maintains invariant expert selection within an $\ell_\infty$ perturbation ball, and the selected expert is robust within that ball, then the MoE system is robust.}
\end{quote}

This decomposition is the key insight: rather than verifying the intractable full system, we verify components independently and compose guarantees. The practical impact is a \textbf{20$\times$ reduction} in verification complexity (2M $\to$ 96K parameters in our experiments).

A \textit{heterogeneous MoE} combines domain-specific experts (potentially with different architectures or training data) coordinated by a learned router. Unlike homogeneous MoEs where experts share structure, heterogeneous systems require the router to perform domain identification---a distinct task from within-domain classification.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compositional Verification Framework:} We formalize conditions under which MoE robustness decomposes into router stability and expert robustness, enabling \textbf{20$\times$ verification speedup} and incremental expert integration without re-verifying the full system.
    
    \item \textbf{Expert Dominance Finding:} Expert training determines system robustness (13.1\% adversarial accuracy difference); router adversarial training has negligible impact ($<$0.1\%) for visually dissimilar domains, enabling 4$\times$ training speedup.
    
    \item \textbf{Verification-Aware Design:} We identify architectural requirements for tractable MoE verification and demonstrate that adversarial training is a \textit{prerequisite} for formal verifiability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Mixture-of-Experts.} The MoE paradigm \cite{jacobs1991} decomposes problems into specialized subtasks via gating networks. Shazeer et al.~\cite{shazeer2017} scaled this through sparse top-$k$ routing, and Riquelme et al.~\cite{riquelme2021} extended MoE to vision. Recent work explores heterogeneous compositions \cite{fedus2023} and modular architectures \cite{pfeiffer2023}, but formal robustness properties remain unaddressed.

\paragraph{Neural Network Verification.} Complete verifiers like $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN \cite{wang2021} provide sound robustness certificates via bound propagation and branch-and-bound. Certified training methods (CROWN-IBP \cite{zhang2020}, SABR \cite{mueller2023}) improve verifiability by training with formal bounds. Randomized smoothing \cite{cohen2019} offers probabilistic certificates for larger models. However, all approaches face scalability limits with multi-million parameter systems.

\paragraph{Compositional Verification.} Compositional approaches decompose verification into manageable subproblems. In robotics and hybrid systems, assume-guarantee reasoning enables modular certificates \cite{henzinger1998}. For neural networks, modular verification remains nascent. MoEs are uniquely suited to compositional verification because the router explicitly partitions inputs into disjoint expert domains---if this partitioning is stable under perturbation, component guarantees compose directly.

\paragraph{Research Gap.} Prior work addresses homogeneous MoE scalability or monolithic neural network verification. We provide the first compositional verification framework for heterogeneous MoE systems, bridging modular architecture design with formal robustness guarantees.

\section{Compositional Verification Framework}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be an input with label $y$. A heterogeneous MoE consists of $N$ domain-specific experts $\{E_i: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{C_i}\}_{i=1}^N$ and a router $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^N$ selecting expert $m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$. We consider $\ell_\infty$-bounded perturbations $B_\varepsilon(x) = \{x': \|x' - x\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\}$.

\subsection{Main Result}

\begin{theorem}[Compositional Robustness]
\label{thm:compositional}
Let $\mathrm{MoE} = (\mathcal{G}, \{E_i\})$ use top-1 routing and let
$m^* = \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x)$.
If
\[
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*
\quad\text{(router stability)}
\]
and
\[
\forall x' \in B_\varepsilon(x): \arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y
\quad\text{(expert robustness),}
\]
then the MoE is $\varepsilon$-robust at $x$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Intuition.}
The router partitions the input space into disjoint expert regions; if these partitions are stable under perturbation and the selected expert is robust within its region, the entire system inherits robustness.

\begin{proof}
For any $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$: router stability guarantees $\arg\max_i \mathcal{G}_i(x') = m^*$, so expert $E_{m^*}$ processes $x'$. Expert robustness guarantees $\arg\max E_{m^*}(x') = y$. Since MoE output depends solely on the selected expert's prediction, the system output remains $y$ for all $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Top-1 Requirement]
Theorem~\ref{thm:compositional} requires top-1 routing. For top-$k$ routing ($k > 1$), the weighted combination of multiple experts introduces additional attack surface: adversaries could exploit the weighting mechanism even if individual experts are robust. Extending compositional guarantees to top-$k$ requires verifying both expert selection stability \textit{and} weight stability---a stronger condition we leave to future work.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Practical Impact.} This decomposition transforms an intractable problem (verifying 2M+ parameter MoE) into manageable subproblems (verifying 96K parameter components). Verification cost scales with the \textit{largest component}, not the full system. New experts integrate without re-verifying existing components.

\subsection{Verification Implementation}

We use $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN for component verification:

\textbf{Router Verification:} For input $x$ with selection $m^*$, verify $\mathcal{G}_{m^*}(x') - \mathcal{G}_j(x') > 0$ for all $j \neq m^*$ and $x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. This reduces to $N-1$ binary properties per input.

\textbf{Expert Verification:} Standard robustness verification---verify the true class logit exceeds all others within $B_\varepsilon(x)$.

\textbf{Challenges:} Router verification difficulty depends on decision margin geometry. When domains are visually dissimilar (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs MNIST), routers learn large margins ($\gg \varepsilon$), enabling easy certification. Similar domains produce smaller margins and harder verification (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Verification-Aware Architecture}

Both router and experts use \textbf{VerifiableCNN} (96K parameters) designed for $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN: average pooling (linear, unlike max pooling which introduces additional branching), no BatchNorm (folded into weights), minimal operators. Training uses PGD adversarial training ($\varepsilon = 8/255$) for robust variants. Full specifications in Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}.

\section{Experiments}

We evaluate on CIFAR-10 and MNIST---visually dissimilar domains enabling clean isolation of compositional effects. Extended experiments on similar domains appear in Appendix~\ref{app:similar_domains}.

\subsection{Router Verification Enables Compositional Guarantees}

& \multicolumn{3}{c}{\textbf{Certified Routing Accuracy (\%)}} \\
\textbf{Training} & $\varepsilon = 2/255$ & $\varepsilon = 4/255$ & $\varepsilon = 8/255$ \\
\midrule
Non-Robust (NRT) & 100 & 100 & 60 \\
Robust (RT) & 100 & 100 & 100 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Router verification (40 samples, 5 runs). Both achieve 100\% at practical threat models ($\varepsilon \leq 4/255$). No falsifications---all failures are timeouts. RT extends certification to larger perturbations.}
\label{tab:router}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key Finding:} Routers achieve high formal certification even without adversarial training at practical $\varepsilon$. The router achieves high certified accuracy because CIFAR-10 and MNIST lie on well-separated data manifolds, giving the router natural decision margins far exceeding $\varepsilon$. The router's empirical adversarial accuracy ($\sim$35\%) appears to contradict formal certification (100\%), but this reflects the distinction between heuristic attack success and worst-case guarantees: PGD finds \textit{some} adversarial examples, while formal verification proves \textit{none exist} that change routing.

\textbf{Key Finding:} Robust training is a \textit{prerequisite} for verifiability, not merely beneficial. Non-robust CIFAR-10 experts produce chaotic decision boundaries that yield loose CROWN bounds and universal verification failure. Adversarial training smooths boundaries, enabling tractable formal analysis.

All configurations achieve \textbf{100\% routing accuracy} (clean and adversarial), validating the compositional property: the router acts as a deterministic switch, accurately transferring expert robustness to the system.

\textbf{Practical Implication:} For dissimilar domains, skip router adversarial training (4$\times$ speedup, 968s vs 4107s) and focus resources on expert robustness.

\subsection{Scalability}

Compositional verification achieves \textbf{20$\times$ parameter reduction} (2M full MoE $\to$ 96K largest component) and \textbf{4.6s average verification time} per sample at practical $\varepsilon$. New experts integrate by verifying only the new component---existing verifications remain valid. Training efficiency improves 16.7\% for 3-expert systems via frozen-expert router fine-tuning.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{When Does Compositional Verification Apply?} The framework requires router stability within $B_\varepsilon(x)$. For dissimilar domains (CIFAR-10 vs MNIST), routers learn large decision margins enabling easy certification. For similar domains (GTSRB vs PTSD traffic signs), routing accuracy degrades to 93\% and system adversarial accuracy drops 12\% (Appendix~\ref{app:similar_domains}). Similar domains may require stronger router training or probabilistic certification; otherwise compositional guarantees degrade.

\paragraph{Architecture Scope.} Our verification-compatible CNNs (96K parameters) demonstrate the framework but limit expressiveness. Scaling to Transformers faces verifier memory constraints. Promising directions include verification-aware architecture search and combining compositional decomposition with randomized smoothing for probabilistic certificates on larger models.

\paragraph{Top-$k$ Extension.} Theorem~\ref{thm:compositional} applies to top-1 routing. Top-$k$ ($k > 1$) requires verifying weight stability in addition to selection stability---a harder condition requiring future work.

\paragraph{Relationship to Empirical Robustness.} Adversarial training improves certifiability by smoothing decision boundaries, reducing bound looseness in CROWN analysis. This explains why robust training simultaneously improves empirical adversarial accuracy and formal certification rates.

% 6. CONCLUSION
%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We establish that MoE robustness composes modularly under top-1 routing: verified router stability combined with verified expert robustness yields verified system robustness. This decomposition achieves 20$\times$ verification speedup and enables incremental expert integration.

Our experiments reveal that expert robustness dominates system behavior (13.1\% impact) while router training is negligible for dissimilar domains ($<$0.1\%). Critically, robust training is prerequisite for formal verifiability---non-robust components are fundamentally incompatible with sound verification.

These results demonstrate that compositional design enables tractable formal verification of modular AI systems, advancing the path toward verifiable, safety-critical deployment.

\section{Training Configuration}
\label{app:hyperparams}

\textbf{Experts:} Adam, LR $10^{-3}$, 200 epochs, batch 128. RT: PGD ($\varepsilon = 8/255$, 7 steps, step $2/255$).

\textbf{Router:} Adam, LR $10^{-3}$, 50 epochs on frozen experts. Same PGD for RT.

\section{Architecture}
\label{app:architecture}

\textbf{VerifiableCNN (96K params):} 4 conv blocks ($3 \to 20 \to 28 \to 40 \to 56$ channels), $3 \times 3$ kernels, ReLU, AvgPool. Global average pooling $\to$ FC(56, 128) $\to$ ReLU $\to$ FC(128, $C$). No BatchNorm (folded for ONNX).

\section{Similar Domain Experiments}
\label{app:similar_domains}

GTSRB + PTSD (both traffic signs): routing accuracy degrades to 93.17\% (vs 100\% for CIFAR-10 + MNIST), adversarial accuracy drops to 29.47\% (vs 41.59\%). The 6.83\% routing error propagates to 12.12\% system accuracy reduction.

Intel i7-14700K, RTX 4060 8GB, 64GB RAM. PyTorch 2.1, $\alpha$--$\beta$-CROWN (Dec 2024), Gurobi 11.0. Timeout: 300s/sample.
